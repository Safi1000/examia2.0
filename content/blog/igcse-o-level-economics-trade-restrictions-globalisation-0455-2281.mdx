Trade restrictions, also called methods of protection, are government measures that limit trade with other countries. IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 name four: **tariffs** (a tax on imports), **import quotas** (a limit on the quantity imported), **subsidies** to home producers and **embargoes** (a ban). Globalisation is the growing connection between countries through trade, firms, people and ideas. Both sit in syllabus section 6.2.

## What does the 2027 syllabus include on free trade and globalisation?

I compared section 6 of the 0455 and 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029. Sections 6.1 and 6.2 are word for word the same, so everything here applies to both. Here is the content as a checklist, in my words.

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 6.1.2 Free trade | Define free trade; give its advantages and disadvantages |
| 6.2.1 Definition of globalisation; 6.2.2 Causes and consequences of changes in globalisation | Define it; explain four causes of changes in it and its effects in six areas |
| 6.2.3 Role of multinational companies (MNCs) | Advantages and disadvantages for host and home countries |
| 6.2.4 Types of trade restrictions / methods of protection | Tariffs, import quotas, subsidies, embargoes |
| 6.2.5 Reasons for trade restrictions | Explain the eight reasons listed |
| 6.2.6 Consequences of trade restrictions | Effects on the home country and its trading partners; advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade |

Section 6.1.1, specialisation by country, sits just before this and feeds the case for free trade.

The changes page in both syllabuses says three things about this area. It lists a new topic on the causes and consequences of changes in globalisation. It lists the definitions of specialisation by country and free trade as added. It also lists reasons for trade restrictions, and the advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade, as added.

On our past paper pages, 96 questions from the 0455 and 2281 papers of 2022 to 2026 carry the 6.2 tag, as main or second tag (0455: 60, 2281: 36).

**Is a tariff diagram on the syllabus?** Section 6.2 does not list one, so I explain the effects of a tariff in words and figures below. Multiple choice can still show a tariff on a graph, though. The 0455 June 2024 Paper 12 question 27 gave a demand and supply diagram for an imported good and asked where the equilibrium would move after a tariff. The supply shift diagrams of section 2.3.3 are all you need for that.

## What is free trade, and what is the case for it?

**Free trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries without restrictions such as tariffs or quotas.** In the June 2022 Paper 23 mark scheme (0455), one mark is for countries exchanging goods and services, or exporting and importing, and one is for doing so without restrictions. An example of a restriction can also earn a mark, up to 2 in all. The report for that paper said some candidates wrote only "trade without restrictions" and so missed full marks, because they never said what was being traded.

Here is the case on each side, in my words.

| Advantages of free trade | Disadvantages of free trade |
|---|---|
| Countries can specialise in what they produce at the lowest cost, so world output rises | Home firms may close if foreign firms are cheaper, causing unemployment |
| Consumers get more choice and lower prices | A country can come to depend on others for vital goods such as food or fuel |
| Firms face more competition, so they push for better quality and efficiency | New industries may never grow big enough to compete |
| Exporters reach bigger markets and may gain economies of scale | Foreign sellers may dump goods below cost |

Free trade and specialisation go together. A country that specialises must trade to get the things it no longer makes.

## What is globalisation, and what causes it to change?

The syllabus lists globalisation as a definition to learn but does not print one. My working definition: **globalisation is the growing connection between the world's economies through trade in goods and services, the movement of firms and people, and the sharing of ideas and technology.**

That lines up with the November 2024 Paper 21 mark scheme (0455). For a question asking for two features of globalisation, it accepted four: migration, trade in goods and services, ideas spreading between countries, and technological development. Firms moving abroad is my addition, taken from the syllabus list of causes.

The syllabus lists four causes of changes in globalisation. Each can push it up or down.

| Cause | How it increases globalisation (reverse it for a decrease) |
|---|---|
| Changes in trade restrictions | Lower tariffs and bigger quotas make imports cheaper and easier, so trade grows |
| Changes in transport costs | Cheaper shipping and flights cut the price of moving goods and people |
| Changes in communication costs | Cheap internet and video calls let firms manage suppliers and staff far away |
| Movement of MNCs | Firms that set up abroad carry money, jobs and technology across borders |

### What are the effects of globalisation?

The syllabus names six areas. These are my explanations.

| Area | Possible effects |
|---|---|
| International trade | More exports and imports; longer supply chains |
| Competition | Home firms face foreign rivals, so prices may fall and quality rise; weak firms may close |
| The environment | More transport and production can raise pollution and use up resources; cleaner technology can also spread faster |
| Migration | More workers move abroad, filling skill gaps in one country and possibly causing a brain drain in another |
| Income distribution | Skilled workers and owners of exporting firms may gain more than unskilled workers whose jobs move abroad, so the gap between rich and poor can widen |
| Economic development | Investment, jobs and technology can raise living standards, though gains may be uneven |

Notice the word "possible". Each effect can go either way, which is why discuss questions on globalisation reward two sides.

## What do multinational companies bring to host and home countries?

An MNC is a firm with its headquarters in one country that produces in other countries. That matches the November 2025 Paper 23 mark scheme (0455), which gave one mark for being based in one country and one for operating in another, and no mark for just naming a company.

The **host country** is where the MNC sets up. The **home country** is where it has its headquarters.

| | Possible advantages | Possible disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Host country | Jobs, tax revenue, new skills and technology, exports | Profits sent home; local firms driven out; natural resources used up |
| Home country | Profits flowing back, more exports, access to raw materials | Jobs moved abroad; less investment at home |

Two report comments are worth knowing. In the 0455 June 2025 Paper 21, question 5(a) asked for two benefits an MNC may provide for its home country. The report called it the hardest of the 2 mark questions on that paper. Many answers earned only 1 mark or none, and reputation was a frequent wrong answer; the scheme does not list it. The scheme accepted points such as profits, exports, raw materials and jobs.

In the 0455 March 2025 Paper 22, question 1(g) asked whether foreign MNCs reduce poverty in host countries. The report said some candidates claimed MNCs might pay less, but never asked whether that pay, though perhaps below wages in the MNC's home country, could still beat local wages.

The 2027 specimen Paper 2 question 5(d) asks whether attracting more foreign MNCs will reduce inflation. One point in its scheme ties back to trade: if MNCs make goods inside the country, it may need to buy fewer imports whose prices tariffs had pushed up.

## What are the four methods of protection?

| Method | What it is | How it cuts imports |
|---|---|---|
| Tariff | A tax on imports | Raises the price of imports, so people buy fewer |
| Import quota | A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported | Caps the amount that can come in |
| Subsidy | A payment from the government to home producers | Lowers home firms' costs, so their goods can compete on price |
| Embargo | A complete ban on imports of a product, or from a country | Stops those imports entirely |

For the June 2022 Paper 23 question asking to explain two methods (0455), the scheme gave 1 mark for naming each method and 1 for explaining it. Most candidates did well, but the report noted that some answers listed two methods and never said how either one works.

**Dumping** appears among the reasons for protection, so learn it too. Dumping is selling a product in a foreign country at a price below its cost of production. The June 2022 Paper 22 mark scheme (0455 and 2281) gave a mark each for the sale below cost, for it being in a foreign country and for a purpose such as winning market share, up to 2. Both reports found that a number of candidates who tried that question did not know what dumping was.

### Which way does a quota change work?

This can trip students up in multiple choice. A quota is a **ceiling**. Raising the quota lets **more** imports in. Lowering it lets **fewer** in.

The 0455 June 2023 Paper 12 question 30 asked which policy would improve a persistent current account deficit. The answer was lowering import quotas; 41 per cent chose it, and 30 per cent chose lowering income tax. The report noted that "Changes in import quotas sometimes cause confusion."

The 0455 March 2025 Paper 12 question 30 turned it round: how to reduce a current account surplus. The answer was increasing existing import quotas, chosen by 37 per cent (the report later says 38). The most popular wrong answer, at 27 per cent, was cutting government spending; the report put this down to treating the current account as part of the government's finances. 23 per cent chose devaluing the currency, which would make the surplus bigger, not smaller.

## What does a tariff do to prices, imports and revenue?

Here is my own example with hypothetical figures. It shows who gains and who loses.

> A country imports electric fans at 8,000 rupees each and sells 5,000 a month. Home firms make a similar fan for 9,000 rupees and sell 2,000 a month. The government puts a 20% tariff on imported fans. Assume importers pass the whole tariff on to buyers. After the tariff, imports fall to 3,000 a month and home fan sales rise to 3,500 a month.

**Step 1. The tariff per fan.** 20% of 8,000 = 1,600 rupees. The imported fan now costs 8,000 + 1,600 = 9,600 rupees.

**Step 2. Government revenue.** 3,000 fans × 1,600 = 4,800,000 rupees a month.

**Step 3. Money paid to foreign producers.**

| | Before the tariff | After the tariff |
|---|---|---|
| Imported fans sold | 5,000 | 3,000 |
| Paid to foreign producers (× 8,000) | 40,000,000 | 24,000,000 |
| Tariff paid to government | 0 | 4,800,000 |
| Total spent on imported fans | 40,000,000 | 28,800,000 |

Check: 24,000,000 + 4,800,000 = 28,800,000, which is 3,000 × 9,600.

**Step 4. Home producers.** Revenue rises from 2,000 × 9,000 = 18,000,000 to 3,500 × 9,000 = 31,500,000 rupees, a rise of 13,500,000.

**Step 5. Consumers.** Total fans bought fall from 7,000 to 6,500. Total spending rises from 40,000,000 + 18,000,000 = 58,000,000 to 28,800,000 + 31,500,000 = 60,300,000 rupees. Consumers pay 2,300,000 more for 500 fewer fans. The cheapest fan on sale rises from 8,000 to 9,000 rupees.

So the tariff helps home firms and the government, cuts the money paid to foreign producers by 16,000,000 a month, and costs consumers. Total spending on imported fans falls by less, 11,200,000, because 4,800,000 of it now goes to the government.

**What if the government used a quota instead?** Suppose it capped imports at 3,000 fans. Imports would fall by the same amount, but the government would collect no tariff revenue. With fewer imported fans available, sellers could raise their price, so that extra money would go to importers rather than the government. That comparison is my own reasoning, but it is a useful evaluation point when a question asks you to choose between methods.

## Why do governments restrict trade?

The syllabus lists eight reasons. Each line is my short explanation.

| Reason | Why it may justify protection |
|---|---|
| Protect infant (sunrise) industries | New firms have high costs until they grow and gain economies of scale |
| Protect declining (sunset) industries | Slows job losses so workers have time to move to other work |
| Protect strategic industries | Keeps vital output, such as food, energy or defence, at home |
| Avoid dumping | Stops foreign firms selling below cost to drive home firms out |
| Reduce a current account deficit | Fewer imports means less spending abroad |
| Raise tax revenue | Tariffs bring in money the government can spend |
| Restrict imports of demerit goods | Cuts consumption of harmful products |
| Promote environmental sustainability | Limits imports made in polluting ways, or of goods that harm the environment |

The March 2022 Paper 22 (0455) asked why an infant industry may need protection. The report rated the answers well overall, singling out points on why new firms struggle to compete at first and how big foreign rivals already in the market could push them out of business.

The June 2023 Paper 22 question 5(c) on reasons for tariffs, shared by 0455 and 2281, also went well, according to the report. It praised what candidates knew about sunrise and sunset industries, and found dumping understood by a fairly high share of them.

The trap is the budget. In November 2022 Paper 22 question 5(b) (0455 and 2281), on why a government may want fewer imports, the report says others mixed up the government's budget balance and the current account balance. In June 2023 Paper 22, the next part, 5(d), asked whether fast growth leads to a current account deficit, and a small proportion confused that deficit with a budget deficit.

That mix up is where I see students lose easy marks. The **current account** records trade and income flows with other countries. The **budget** is the government's own tax and spending. A tariff cuts imports, which helps the current account, and it raises tax revenue, which helps the budget. Keep the two apart.

## What are the consequences of trade restrictions?

Section 6.2.6 asks for effects on the **home country** and on its **trading partners**, and for the advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade.

| Group | Possible effects of a restriction |
|---|---|
| Home producers | More sales, more jobs, more profit; but less pressure to become efficient |
| Home consumers | Higher prices and less choice |
| Home firms using imported materials | Higher costs, which can raise prices and cause inflation |
| Government | Tariff revenue, though not from quotas or embargoes |
| Trading partners | Lower exports, so lost jobs and output in those countries |
| Home exporters | Risk of retaliation: partners may put their own tariffs on the home country's goods |

The 0455 June 2025 Paper 21 question 5(b) asked for two disadvantages of import tariffs. In the report, many candidates saw that a tariff makes imports dearer and may feed cost-push inflation, and less trade and retaliation came up often too. Fewer took those ideas far enough for the explanation mark, and many wrote about the effect on the government instead. The scheme gave 1 mark for each disadvantage and 1 for its explanation.

<Tip title="A restriction is only as strong as demand allows">
A tariff cuts imports a lot only if demand for the import is price elastic and home substitutes exist. If demand is inelastic, people keep buying the import and simply pay more. Use this as your evaluation point in any 8 mark tariff question.
</Tip>

The November 2023 Paper 23 question 2(d), the same paper for 2281 and 0455, asked whether tariffs on imports will increase a country's output. Its mark scheme gave points on each side. On one side: dearer imports, buyers turning to home products, and tariff revenue that could pay for subsidies to home firms. On the other: foreign goods could stay cheaper even with the tariff, there may be no home alternative, tariffs on raw materials push up costs, and retaliation hurts exports.

## How are trade restriction questions marked?

The 2027 Paper 2 is 2 hours and 80 marks. Section A is one compulsory 20 mark question. In Section B you answer three of four questions, each worth 20 marks in four parts. In the specimen Paper 2, those parts are worth 2, 4, 6 and 8 marks.

Here is how past papers marked each type on this topic.

| Question type and marks | Paper | How marks were given |
|---|---|---|
| Define free trade or dumping [2] | 0455 June 2022 P23; June 2022 P22 (0455 and 2281) | Up to 2 marks from the parts of the definition |
| Identify two methods of protection [2] | November 2022 P22 (0455 and 2281) | 1 for each: tariff, quota, subsidy or embargo |
| Explain two methods or two disadvantages [4] | 0455 June 2022 P23; 0455 June 2025 P21 | 1 for each point, 1 for each explanation |
| Analyse reasons for tariffs [6] | June 2023 P22 and November 2022 P23 (0455 and 2281) | 1 mark for each link in the chain |
| Discuss whether protection or tariffs help [8] | 0455 June 2022 P23; November 2023 P23 (0455 and 2281) | Levels: both sides developed for the top level |

For part (d), the specimen Table A gives 6 to 8 marks when both sides are weighed with clear analysis, 3 to 5 when the answer is thinner or leans on one side, and 1 to 2 for definitions or points left unexplained. That is my summary of the table, not its wording.

Paper 1 tests this area too. In the 2027 specimen Paper 1 (0455 and 2281), question 38 asked which option is **not** a reason for trade restrictions. The key is the option about shielding consumers from high prices set by a home monopoly. Protection does the opposite: it shelters home firms from foreign rivals.

## What mistakes do examiners report?

| Mistake (session and paper) | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Defining free trade only as trade with no restrictions (some, 0455 June 2022 P23) | Say what is traded: goods and services exchanged between countries |
| Naming methods of protection without explaining them (some answers, 0455 June 2022 P23) | Name the method, then say how it cuts imports |
| Writing "taxes" without saying what is taxed (some, November 2022 P22 5(a), 0455 and 2281) | Write "a tax on imports", or "tariff" |
| Not knowing the meaning of dumping (a number, June 2022 P22, 0455 and 2281) | Selling abroad below the cost of production |
| Confusing the budget with the current account (others, November 2022 P22 5(b); a small proportion, June 2023 P22 5(d), a current account question; 27 per cent chose a budget answer, 0455 March 2025 P12 Q30) | Current account is trade with other countries; budget is government tax and spending |
| Giving one tariff reason without explaining it, such as just saying the country will get more money (weak answers, November 2022 P23 5(c), 0455 and 2281) | Name the reason and develop it: revenue that can be spent on, say, schools |
| Writing about the government when asked for disadvantages of tariffs (many, 0455 June 2025 P21) | Focus on consumers, firms, trade and retaliation |
| Mixing up tariffs with quotas in a tariff question (a few, 2281 November 2023 P23) | A tariff changes price; a quota limits quantity |

## Practice questions with answers

All three are my own, with hypothetical figures.

### Practice question 1

> A country imports 20,000 rice cookers a month at $50 each. The government puts a 30% tariff on imported rice cookers, and imports fall to 14,000 a month.
>
> (a) Calculate the price of an imported rice cooker after the tariff, assuming the whole tariff is passed on. [1]
>
> (b) Calculate the monthly tariff revenue. [2]
>
> (c) Calculate the fall in money paid to foreign rice cooker producers each month. [2]

**Answer**

(a) 30% of $50 = $15, so the price is $50 + $15 = $65.

(b) 14,000 × $15 = $210,000.

(c) Before: 20,000 × $50 = $1,000,000. After: 14,000 × $50 = $700,000. The fall is $300,000.

### Practice question 2

> Explain two ways a fall in communication costs could increase globalisation. [4]

**Answer**

Cheaper internet and phone calls let a firm manage factories and suppliers in other countries at low cost **[1]**, so more firms become MNCs and produce abroad **[1]**. Buyers can also find and order from foreign sellers online more easily **[1]**, so trade in goods and services between countries grows **[1]**.

How I would mark it is my own judgement, not a Cambridge scheme: one mark for each way, one for its development.

### Practice question 3

> Discuss whether or not a government should use tariffs to protect a declining (sunset) industry. [8]

**Answer**

*Why it should.* A tariff raises the price of competing imports, so demand switches to the home industry. Its firms keep selling, so jobs are saved for now. This gives older workers time to retrain and move to other work, which may reduce structural unemployment. The tariff also brings in revenue, which could pay for that retraining.

*Why it should not.* The industry is declining, perhaps because foreign producers have lower costs. A tariff only delays the decline, and protected firms may feel no pressure to become efficient. Consumers pay higher prices and have less choice. Firms that buy the product as an input face higher costs. Trading partners may retaliate with tariffs on the country's exports, putting jobs in other industries at risk.

*Judgement.* A short, falling tariff linked to retraining may be justified. A permanent tariff is likely to cost consumers and exporters more than it saves.

My marking: an answer like this, with both sides developed and a reasoned judgement, fits the top level (6 to 8) of the specimen Table A.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a tariff and a quota?

A tariff is a tax on imports, so it raises their price and brings in revenue. A quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. It raises no revenue for the government.

### What is the difference between an embargo and a quota?

A quota limits imports to a set quantity. An embargo bans them completely, either of a product or from a country.

### Is globalisation new in the 2027 syllabus?

The changes page for 0455 and 2281 lists a new topic on the causes and consequences of changes in globalisation. Definitions of free trade and specialisation by country, and the reasons for trade restrictions, are also listed as added.

### Do I need to draw a tariff diagram?

Section 6.2 of the 2027 syllabus does not list one. Explain a tariff's effects in words, with figures if the question gives them. In multiple choice, be ready to read a tariff as a supply shift on a demand and supply diagram.

### What is dumping?

Selling a product in a foreign country at a price below its cost of production. Avoiding dumping is one of the eight reasons for trade restrictions in the syllabus.

### Why might a tariff fail to reduce imports?

If demand for the import is price inelastic, or there is no home substitute, people keep buying it at the higher price. Retaliation can also cut the country's exports.
