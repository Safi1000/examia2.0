A 9708 Paper 4 essay is one 20 mark answer with no parts. The examiner gives up to 14 marks for knowledge and analysis and up to 6 for evaluation, each by levels. To reach the top, write two developed chains of reasoning on each side, use an accurate diagram where it fits, weigh the arguments, and end with a judgement on the exact question.

## How is 9708 Paper 4 set out?

Paper 4 is the A Level data response and essays paper. These facts are from the 9708 syllabus for 2026 to 2028.

| Paper 4 fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Time and marks | 2 hours, 60 marks |
| Weight | 33% of the A Level |
| Section A | One compulsory data response question in four parts, 20 marks |
| Section B | One essay from a choice of two, mainly microeconomics, 20 marks |
| Section C | One essay from a choice of two, mainly macroeconomics, 20 marks |
| Essay format | Unstructured, with no parts |

The questions are on the A Level content, but the AS content is assumed knowledge. So an essay on monopsony still expects you to use demand and supply, elasticity and efficiency from your AS year.

Evaluation matters more on Paper 4 than on the multiple choice paper. The syllabus gives evaluation 30% of Paper 4, against 13% of Paper 3. Knowledge and understanding is 33% and analysis is 37%.

### Can I practise on papers from before 2023?

Yes for content, but not for timing or marking. The March 2023 examiner report explains that the format changed that year. Before 2023, candidates chose any two essays from six, and essays were marked out of 25. Since 2023, you answer one from Section B and one from Section C, each out of 20. The 2022 Paper 4 papers on our site still have the old parts, so mark your practice on them with the tables below, not their own mark schemes.

## How are the 20 marks split?

Every Paper 4 mark scheme I checked, from November 2024 to June 2026, uses the same two tables for the essays. Table A marks knowledge and analysis together. Table B marks evaluation.

| Table A: knowledge and analysis (14 marks) | What the level needs |
|---|---|
| Level 3: 11 to 14 | Thorough knowledge, reasoning taken all the way through, correct diagrams that you explain, and every paragraph aimed at the question |
| Level 2: 6 to 10 | Some of the right ideas, reasoning that is mostly correct but stops early, diagrams with slips or left unexplained |
| Level 1: 1 to 5 | A handful of points, more description than reasoning, weak link to the question |

| Table B: evaluation (6 marks) | What the level needs |
|---|---|
| Level 2: 4 to 6 | A final judgement on the exact question, backed by reasons, plus evaluative comments that you develop and support |
| Level 1: 1 to 3 | A loose, general ending and short evaluative remarks left undeveloped |

The level wording above is my own summary, so read the published tables in any recent mark scheme too. Two things follow from them. First, the examiner reads the whole answer and places it in a level. There is no mark for each point, so ten shallow points can still sit in Level 2. Second, evaluation is marked on its own. A brilliant analysis with a weak ending can score 13 out of 14 and 2 out of 6.

The March 2023 examiner report made one useful point: a fully developed conclusion could earn at least 4 of the 6 evaluation marks. So the conclusion is the most valuable paragraph you write.

## What do the question words mean?

Paper 4 essays use a small set of command words. The syllabus defines each one; the meanings below are my summary of those definitions. In practice, every one of them needs a judgement at the end, because 6 marks depend on it.

| Command word | Syllabus meaning, in my words | What it asks of you |
|---|---|---|
| Assess | Reach a judgement you can support | Weigh both sides, then decide |
| Evaluate | Judge how good, how important or how large something is | Test the claim, then decide how far it holds |
| Discuss | Cover the issues in depth, in an organised way | Two sides in depth, then a conclusion |
| Consider | Look over the information given and respond to it | Respond to the claim and its limits |

Many Paper 4 questions give a statement, then ask you to evaluate it. These are traps for students who agree with the statement. The March 2023 examiner report said that, on the collusion essay, most candidates did not question the statement at all, so very few earned evaluation marks. The statement is there to be tested, word by word.

Look for absolute words: "always", "only", "all", "inevitably", "automatically". They are usually the weak point. Your judgement often becomes "true in these conditions, but not always, because...".

## How do I plan a 20 mark essay?

If you split the 2 hours evenly, you have about 40 minutes per essay. I tell my students to spend the first 5 minutes planning. Both timings are my teaching advice, not a Cambridge rule. A short plan guards against two faults the examiner reports describe: answers that drift away from the question, and conclusions that only summarise.

Use this six step plan.

1. **Underline every part of the question.** Some questions have two or three elements, such as "on wages and employment" or "trade and aid". Each one needs equal weight.
2. **Write your judgement in one line** before you start. Every paragraph should lead towards it.
3. **Define the key terms** in two or three sentences. No general introduction.
4. **The case for**: two developed chains of reasoning, with a diagram if it helps or is asked for.
5. **The case against**: two developed chains, as detailed as the case for.
6. **Weigh and conclude**: say what the answer depends on, which argument matters more and when, then answer the exact words of the question.

Steps 3 to 5 earn Table A marks. Step 6, and the short "this depends on" comments inside steps 4 and 5, earn Table B marks.

### What does a developed chain look like?

The Level 3 descriptor asks for analysis that is "developed and detailed". The March 2023 report noted that many answers were based on "a simple chain of reasoning", which held them in Level 2. A developed chain runs at least three links, and says why it matters for the question.

| Simple (Level 2) | Developed (Level 3) |
|---|---|
| A minimum wage in a monopsony raises employment. | A monopsonist pays a wage below the marginal revenue product of labour, because hiring one more worker raises the wage for all of them. A minimum wage set between that wage and the competitive wage makes the marginal cost of labour constant up to the supply curve. The firm now hires more workers at the higher wage, so both pay and employment can rise. |

### When must I draw a diagram?

When the question says "with the help of a diagram", the diagram decides your level. The June 2023 and November 2023 examiner reports for Paper 41 said answers without a correct, labelled diagram would not gain marks beyond Level 2. The March 2025 report repeated it: where a diagram is asked for, a correct one is essential for Level 3.

When the question does not ask, the syllabus still encourages clearly labelled diagrams where they fit. The 2023 reports gave examples, such as AD/AS diagrams in macro essays.

<Tip title="Use the diagram in your writing">
After drawing it, refer to it by its labels: "employment rises from L1 to L2". The June 2023 and November 2023 reports both criticised perfect diagrams that the essay never mentioned.
</Tip>

## A marked 20 mark sample

This question and answer are my own, in the style of recent papers. The tags show which skill each part displays: K for knowledge, An for analysis, Ev for evaluation. All figures are invented for the example.

**Question:** "Replacing means tested benefits with a universal basic income is the best way for a government to reduce poverty." Evaluate this statement. [20]

**Answer**

A means tested benefit is paid only to people whose income or wealth is below a set level, and it is reduced as their income rises. A universal basic income (UBI) is a regular payment of the same amount to every adult, whatever their income, with no conditions **[K]**. Poverty can be absolute, where a person cannot afford basic needs, or relative, where their income is well below the typical income in their society **[K]**.

The strongest case for UBI is that it removes the poverty trap. With means tested benefits, a low paid worker who earns more loses benefit as well as paying tax **[K]**. Suppose benefit is withdrawn at 50 cents for each extra dollar earned and income tax is 20%. If the worker earns an extra $100, they lose $50 of benefit and pay $20 in tax, so they keep only $30. The effective marginal tax rate is 70% **[An]**. This weakens the incentive to work longer hours or take a better paid job, so people stay dependent on benefits and stay poor **[An]**. A UBI is not withdrawn as income rises, so the worker keeps the extra $100 minus tax. Work always pays, which raises labour supply among low income households **[An]**.

UBI also reaches everyone in poverty. Means tested benefits often go unclaimed, because people do not know they qualify, find the forms difficult, or feel ashamed to apply **[An]**. A UBI needs no application, so take up is complete and the cost of checking incomes falls **[An]**.

However, UBI is expensive, because most of the money goes to people who are not poor **[K]**. Take an economy with 10 million adults. A UBI of $100 a month costs $1,000 million a month, or $12,000 million a year **[An]**. To pay for it, the government must raise taxes, borrow, or cut other spending such as health and education. Higher income tax could reduce the incentive to work for middle and high earners, which works against the labour supply gain above **[An]**. If the government cannot afford a UBI large enough to cover basic needs, it may leave the poorest worse off than a well targeted benefit would **[An]**.

Means tested benefits also have a strength: they target help where it is needed. For each dollar spent, more reaches poor households, so relative poverty can fall faster for a given budget **[An]**.

Whether UBI is the best way depends first on the government's budget **[Ev]**. A high income country with a broad tax base may afford a UBI at a useful level. A lower income country with a small tax base and a large informal economy probably cannot, and targeted help is likely to do more for the poorest **[Ev]**. It also depends on the type of poverty. If poverty is mainly among people who cannot work, such as the elderly or disabled, the work incentive gain from UBI matters little, while targeted payments meet their needs directly **[Ev]**.

Overall, the statement is too strong. UBI is a better way to reduce poverty among low paid workers caught in the poverty trap, because it keeps the reward for working. It is not the best way for every government, because its cost is high and much of it goes to people who are not poor. For most governments, a reform that lowers the withdrawal rate of means tested benefits may achieve much of the work incentive at a lower cost. UBI is the best option only where the government can fund it at a level that covers basic needs **[Ev]**.

**How I would mark it**

These marks are my own judgement as a teacher, using the two tables above. They are not a Cambridge mark.

| Table | Level and marks | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge and analysis (out of 14) | Level 3, 13 marks | Precise definitions, two developed chains on each side, a worked poverty trap figure and a costing |
| Evaluation (out of 6) | Level 2, 5 marks | Depends on the budget and the type of poverty, then a justified judgement on "the best way" |
| Total | 18 out of 20 | |

Check the sums: $50 lost plus $20 tax is $70 of the extra $100, so the worker keeps $30. Ten million adults at $100 is $1,000 million a month, and twelve months gives $12,000 million.

Why not 20? A Lorenz curve or a labour supply diagram could have made the analysis fully detailed. The answer could also have used a real economy as an example, which the Table A Level 3 descriptor rewards where it fits.

Notice what the conclusion does. It does not repeat the paragraphs. It answers "best way" directly, names the condition that decides it, and offers a better alternative. That is a judgement, not a summary.

### What would a typical answer score?

Picture a weaker version. It explains the poverty trap well, lists four benefits of UBI, and ends: "In conclusion, UBI has many advantages but also some disadvantages." In my judgement that is Level 2 for analysis, perhaps 8 or 9 out of 14, because the points are not developed and the other side is missing. The conclusion is vague, so evaluation stays in Level 1 at about 2 out of 6. That is around 11 out of 20 from a student who knew the topic.

## How do I plan a macro essay in 5 minutes?

Section C works the same way. Here is a plan, not a full answer, for my own question on the Phillips curve. Use it as a model of what your 5 minute plan should contain.

**Question:** Evaluate whether a government that wants lower unemployment must always accept a higher rate of inflation. [20]

**Judgement line:** Only in the short run, and only when the economy is near capacity.

| Plan step | What I would write |
|---|---|
| Key terms | Unemployment rate; inflation; the traditional Phillips curve as an inverse link between the two |
| Case for | Diagram: inflation on the vertical axis, unemployment on the horizontal, a curve sloping down. Higher aggregate demand cuts unemployment, labour becomes scarce, wages and prices rise faster |
| Case against | Expectations augmented Phillips curve: once workers expect higher inflation, the short run curve shifts up and unemployment returns to its natural rate, shown by a vertical long run curve. Supply side policy, such as training, can lower the natural rate without extra inflation |
| Depends on | How close the economy is to full capacity; whether expectations adjust quickly; the type of unemployment (cyclical or structural) |
| Conclusion | Not always. The trade off holds in the short run for demand side policy near capacity. In the long run there is no stable trade off, and supply side policy can lower unemployment without accepting higher inflation |

Notice the word "always" in the question. The plan tests it directly, and the conclusion repeats it.

## Where do I find evaluation points?

Evaluation is not a separate list of facts. It is a question you ask of your own analysis: is this always true, and what does it depend on? These are the lenses that work across the A Level topics. Each one appears in recent Paper 4 mark schemes or examiner reports.

| Topic area | It depends on |
|---|---|
| Consumer theory (utility, indifference curves) | Whether the model's assumptions hold, such as rational consumers with full information. The June 2026 mark scheme listed rationality and the two good world as limits |
| Market structures and collusion | Economies of scale, investment and government action. The March 2023 report named these as reasons collusion may not harm consumers |
| Labour markets | The elasticity of demand for and supply of labour, and how easily capital replaces labour (March 2025 report). Whether the market is competitive or monopsony |
| Trade unions and minimum wages | Productivity agreements that raise the demand for labour (June 2026 mark scheme) |
| Market failure policy | Government failure, and whether the aim is efficiency or equity. The March 2026 report expected both to be considered when "equitable" was in the question |
| Policies that cut spending and unemployment | The state of the economy when the policy starts, and the type of unemployment (March 2023 report) |
| Inflation targets and expectations | How people's expectations respond (March 2026 report) |
| Money and inflation | Whether velocity and output really stay constant (June 2026 mark scheme) |
| Exchange rates and tariffs | The Marshall Lerner condition and the J curve, retaliation, and the welfare loss from tariffs (March 2025 report) |
| Policy over time | Short run against long run effects. The March 2025 report expected this distinction for supply side policies |
| Globalisation and development | Who gains and who loses: resource depletion, environmental damage, labour exploitation (March 2023 report) |
| Living standards | Measurement problems, the informal economy, and other measures such as the HDI or MPI (June 2026 mark scheme) |

Two habits turn these into marks. First, attach each lens to your own analysis, not to the topic in general. "This depends on elasticity" earns little. "Because demand for labour in this industry is inelastic, the job losses are small" earns more. Second, develop each comment. The March 2025 report said brief one sentence statements usually kept candidates at Level 1 for evaluation.

<Tip title="Rank, do not list">
In your conclusion, say which factor matters most and why. "It depends on the budget first, and the type of poverty second" is a judgement. "It depends on many factors" is not.
</Tip>

## Where do students lose marks on 9708 Paper 4 essays?

These come from the 9708 Paper 4 examiner reports for March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, March 2025 and March 2026.

| Mistake | What the examiners reported | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Agreeing with the statement | On one essay, most did not question the statement after "Evaluate", so very few earned evaluation marks (March 2023) | Test every absolute word in the statement |
| Analysis passed off as evaluation | Analysing a policy's impact was not evaluation; questioning its extent was (March 2023) | Ask "how far?" and "when not?" after each chain |
| Missing a key word | Answers on the rate of inflation when the question asked about a target rate; efficiency only when "equitable" was asked (March 2026) | Underline and use the key word in every paragraph |
| Wrong market or wrong level | Perfect competition diagrams for a monopsony question; macro points in a micro question (March 2025) | Check which market and which section the question is in |
| Learned answers | Answers learned in advance that did not match the question set (June 2023, November 2023) | Build the answer from the question's words, not your notes |
| Long but unfocused | Very long answers were often poorly directed; concise, relevant answers are rewarded (June 2023, November 2023) | Two developed chains each side, not six thin ones |
| Unequal balance | Weaker responses did not balance trade and aid (March 2026) | Give each element in the question equal depth |
| Summary conclusions | Conclusions that summarised rather than judged (March 2025, March 2026) | Rank the arguments and answer the exact question |

## How long should each part of Paper 4 take?

Paper 4 gives 2 hours for 60 marks, so about 2 minutes per mark. Each section is worth 20 marks, which is about 40 minutes. This is the split I teach, not a Cambridge rule.

| Part | Time |
|---|---|
| Section A data response | 40 minutes |
| Section B essay: plan, then write | 5 minutes plus 35 minutes |
| Section C essay: plan, then write | 5 minutes plus 35 minutes |

You can do the essays first, while you are fresh, then return to the data response. That is fine if you keep to the 40 minute blocks.

## Practice questions

Write a 5 minute plan for each one first, using the six steps. Then check it against the answers below.

**1.** With the help of a diagram, assess whether nudge policies are more effective than indirect taxes in reducing the consumption of a demerit good. [20]

**2.** "Cutting the rate of income tax will always increase a government's total tax revenue." Evaluate this statement. [20]

**3.** A student ends an essay like this: "In conclusion, there are arguments on both sides, so it depends on the situation." Rewrite the conclusion for question 2 so it could reach Level 2 for evaluation.

### Answers

**1.** Key terms: a demerit good, a nudge (a change in how choices are presented that steers behaviour without banning options or changing prices), an indirect tax. Diagram: a tax shifts the supply curve up by the tax per unit, price rises and quantity falls towards the socially optimal level. Case for taxes: a certain price signal, and revenue to fund treatment. Case for nudges: cheap, and they act on the habits and poor information behind the consumption, where a tax on price inelastic demand changes little. Depends on: price elasticity of demand, how strong the habit is, and the risk of government failure. Judgement: neither alone is best; a tax works better when demand is elastic, a nudge when consumption is driven by habit or poor information.

**2.** Key terms: the income tax rate, total tax revenue, the Laffer curve. Case for: on the Laffer curve, above the rate that maximises revenue, a cut raises the incentive to work and declare income, so revenue rises. Case against: below that rate, a cut lowers revenue, because the tax base does not grow enough to make up for the lower rate. Depends on: the starting tax rate, how responsive labour supply is, and how much tax is avoided or evaded. Judgement: not always; only when the starting rate is above the revenue maximising rate.

**3.** A Level 2 version: "A cut in the income tax rate raises total revenue only when the starting rate is above the revenue maximising point on the Laffer curve. In most economies with moderate rates, labour supply responds only a little, so a cut is more likely to reduce revenue. The statement is therefore not true in general. It holds only for an economy with very high tax rates and widespread avoidance." It names the deciding condition, weighs how likely each case is, and answers "always".

## FAQ

### How many paragraphs should a 20 mark Economics essay have?

There is no set number. A definition paragraph, two on each side and a weighing and conclusion paragraph, about six in all, fits the levels well in 35 minutes of writing.

### Is the 9708 Paper 4 essay marked like the AS essays?

No. AS Paper 2 essays have two parts, 8 and 12 marks. Paper 4 essays are one 20 mark answer, marked out of 14 for knowledge and analysis and out of 6 for evaluation.

### Can I get evaluation marks without a conclusion?

You can earn Level 1 with simple evaluative comments. Level 2, 4 to 6 marks, needs a justified conclusion that addresses the specific question.

### Do I need to draw a diagram in every Paper 4 essay?

When the question asks for one, yes: without a correct, labelled diagram you cannot go beyond Level 2. When it is not asked for, draw one if it helps your analysis, and explain it.

### Do I need real world examples?

The Level 3 descriptor in Table A rewards explanations backed by examples where they fit. A short, relevant example helps, but it does not replace analysis.

### Should I use AS content in Paper 4?

Yes. The syllabus says AS content is assumed knowledge for Paper 4. Elasticity, AD/AS and market failure from your AS year often carry the analysis in A Level essays.
