In Economics Paper 2, knowing the content is only half the job. The other half is the shape of your answer. They write a definition when the question wants a chain of reasoning, or one side when it wants two. Once you know what each command word asks for and how the marks are split, your answers get shorter and score higher.

## How Paper 2 is built

In IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281, Section B gives you four questions and you answer three. Each is worth 20 marks and has four parts. In recent papers those parts follow the same pattern:

| Part | Marks | Usual command word | Skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | 2 | Define, Identify | Knowledge |
| (b) | 4 | Explain | Knowledge and application |
| (c) | 6 | Analyse | Analysis |
| (d) | 8 | Discuss | Analysis and evaluation |

You can see this pattern in O Level 2281/21 (May/June 2024), where every Section B question runs (a) 2, (b) 4, (c) 6, (d) 8, and in the specimen Paper 2 for 2027. Section A is a compulsory data question with the same command words at different mark values.

One change is coming. For exams in 2026, Paper 2 is 2 hours 15 minutes and 90 marks. From 2027 it becomes 2 hours and 80 marks, with Section A cut to 20 marks. Section B keeps its four parts.

## What the command words mean

These are Cambridge's own definitions from the 0455 and 2281 syllabuses:

| Command word | Cambridge's meaning |
|---|---|
| Define | give precise meaning |
| Explain | set out purposes or reasons / make the relationships between things clear / say why and/or how and support with relevant evidence |
| Analyse | examine in detail to show meaning, identify elements and the relationship between them |
| Discuss | write about issue(s) or topic(s) in depth in a structured way |

**What about "evaluate"?** It is not on the command word list for 0455 or 2281. Evaluation is a skill (assessment objective AO3), and Paper 2 tests it mainly through "Discuss" questions. The 2027 syllabus says part (d) answers must "consider two sides of economic arguments". When your teacher says "evaluate", they mean: weigh both sides, and say what the answer depends on.

## 2 marks: Define

**How the marks work.** Two marks for a precise meaning. In the mark schemes, the two marks go either to two separate parts of the definition, or to the meaning plus an example. In 2281/21 (May/June 2024) Question 4(a), "Define tertiary sector using an example", one mark was for the meaning and one for the example.

**Structure:** one sentence with every key part of the definition, plus an example if the question asks for one.

**Example question:** Define "opportunity cost". [2]

> Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. For example, if the government spends on a new road, the hospital it could have built instead is the opportunity cost.

Notice "next best". A student who writes "what you give up" has the general idea, but "precise meaning" needs the full definition.

## 4 marks: Explain two …

**How the marks work.** For "Explain two" questions, the mark schemes award one mark for identifying each point and one mark for explaining it, two times over. That is the split in the 2027 specimen mark scheme: "1 mark for identification and 1 mark for explanation (× 2)".

**Structure:** two short paragraphs. Each one states a point, then says **why** or **how** with "because" or "so".

**Example question:** Explain two reasons why the demand for air travel in a country may increase. [4]

> First, incomes may rise. Air travel is a normal good, so when households have more disposable income they can afford more holidays abroad and buy more flights.
>
> Second, the price of a substitute, such as long-distance train travel, may rise. Train travel then becomes relatively more expensive, so some travellers switch to flying instead.

Two points, each explained once. A third point earns nothing extra.

## 6 marks: Analyse

**How the marks work.** The 6 marks reward a chain of reasoning. In the 2027 specimen, "Analyse two policy measures" gives up to 2 marks for naming two policies and the rest for analysis. In 2281/21 (May/June 2024) Question 5(c), the guidance gave "a maximum of three marks for only identifying the reasons". Naming without linking caps your score at half.

**Structure:** cause → effect → effect → final outcome. Each arrow is a link the examiner can credit. Use "this leads to", "as a result", "which means".

**Example question:** Analyse how a rise in the rate of interest may reduce inflation. [6]

> A rise in the rate of interest increases the cost of borrowing. Households take out fewer loans for cars and houses, so consumer expenditure falls. Firms also find it more expensive to borrow for new machinery, so investment falls.
>
> Saving also becomes more rewarding, so households save more of their income and spend less of it.
>
> As consumer expenditure and investment fall, total (aggregate) demand in the economy falls. With less demand for goods and services, firms are less able to raise their prices, so the general price level rises more slowly. This means the rate of inflation falls.

Count the links in that answer: borrowing cost, loans, consumption, investment, saving, total demand, price level, inflation. That is a full chain.

<Tip>
Draw a diagram when it helps you show the chain, for example a fall in total demand or a shift in a supply curve. Label both axes, every curve and both equilibrium points. Then refer to the diagram in your writing. An unlabelled diagram, or one you never mention, adds very little.
</Tip>

## 8 marks: Discuss whether or not …

**How the marks work.** Discuss questions are marked in levels, not point by point. In the specimen mark scheme for 2027, the levels are:

| Level | Marks | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 6 to 8 | A reasoned discussion of two sides. One side may have more depth, but both are developed. |
| 2 | 3 to 5 | Clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided. |
| 1 | 1 to 2 | Relevant definitions or statements that are not developed. |

So a one-sided answer, however good, is capped at Level 2. To reach Level 3, both sides must be developed.

**Structure:**

1. **Why it might**: one or two developed points, each a short chain.
2. **Why it might not**: one or two developed points, each a short chain.
3. **Judgement**: one or two sentences on what the answer depends on.

**Example question:** Discuss whether or not a government should raise the national minimum wage. [8]

> **Why it should.** A higher minimum wage raises the income of the lowest-paid workers. This can reduce poverty and narrow the gap between rich and poor. Low-paid workers spend most of any extra income, so consumer expenditure rises, which increases total demand and can create jobs in other industries. Better pay may also motivate workers, raising their productivity and reducing labour turnover for firms.
>
> **Why it should not.** If the minimum wage is set above the market wage, firms' costs of production rise. Firms may demand fewer workers, so unemployment can rise, especially among young and unskilled workers. Some firms may pass the higher costs on to consumers as higher prices, which adds to inflation. Firms competing with imports may lose sales to cheaper foreign producers.
>
> **Judgement.** Whether the government should raise it depends on how large the rise is and on the state of the economy. A small rise when the economy is growing and firms are profitable is likely to help low-paid workers with little loss of jobs. A large rise during a recession is more likely to increase unemployment.

The "Why it should not" paragraph is a separate argument. It is not "Why it should" turned upside down. Mark schemes warn against that: 2281/21 (May/June 2024) Question 5(d) says "Do not reward a simple reversal".

## Mistakes that cost marks

1. **Listing in an Explain question.** "Higher income, lower price of flights" is two identifications with no explanation: 2 out of 4 at most.
2. **Writing more points than asked.** The mark schemes only look at the first few points, so a weak first point is not rescued by a strong fifth one. For example, the 2027 specimen says "only consider the first three" for an Explain two question. Put your best points first.
3. **Mirror statements.** "Firms gain more revenue" followed by "firms do not lose revenue" is the same point twice. Cambridge's marking principles say not to credit mirror statements.
4. **Key terms without meaning.** The marking principles say not to credit answers simply for using a key term. Show what the term does in your chain.
5. **Ignoring the extract in Section A.** The June 2024 examiner report for 2281 notes that many candidates based answers on information not included in the extract. If the question says "in Nicaragua" or "using the information", the evidence must come from the source.
6. **Answering out of order.** The same report noted more candidates answering parts out of order and writing the wrong question number. Number every part clearly, in the order on the paper.
7. **One-sided Discuss answers.** Two developed sides, then a judgement, every time.

## How to practise

Pick one Section B question a day. Before writing, plan it in 60 seconds: the two points for (b), the chain for (c), both sides for (d). Then write it, and mark it against the official mark scheme. Within two weeks the structures become automatic, and you stop running out of time on the 8 marker.
