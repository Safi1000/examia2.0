Price elasticity of demand answers one question: when the price changes, how much does the quantity people buy change? Firms use it to decide whether a price rise will bring in more money or less. Governments use it to work out whether a tax will raise revenue or cut consumption. Once you can calculate it and say what the number means, a whole group of exam questions opens up.

The 0455 syllabus lists the calculation of PED with its formula, interpreting the result, the link between PED and total revenue, and why PED matters.

## The formula

**PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price**

And each percentage change is:

**percentage change = change ÷ original value × 100**

Always divide by the **original** value, the one before the change. Using the new value is the most common way to get the right method and the wrong answer.

## Two worked examples

### A bus fare

A bus company raises its fare from $2.00 to $2.50. Passengers a day fall from 1,000 to 900.

1. Change in price: $2.50 − $2.00 = $0.50. Percentage: 0.50 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = **+25%**.
2. Change in quantity: 900 − 1,000 = −100. Percentage: −100 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **−10%**.
3. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = **−0.4**.

### A cinema ticket

A cinema raises its ticket price from $8 to $10. Tickets sold a night fall from 500 to 350.

1. Price: 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = **+25%**.
2. Quantity: −150 ÷ 500 × 100 = **−30%**.
3. PED = −30 ÷ 25 = **−1.2**.

Same price rise in percentage terms, very different response. That difference is the whole point of the measure.

## What the number means

PED is negative for almost every good, because a higher price means people buy less. To decide what type of demand it is, look at the size of the number and ignore the minus sign.

| PED (ignoring the sign) | Demand is | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| More than 1 | Price elastic | Quantity changes by a bigger percentage than price |
| Less than 1 | Price inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| Exactly 1 | Unitary | Quantity changes by the same percentage as price |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity does not change at all |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Any price rise and demand falls to zero |

The bus fare (0.4) is inelastic: people still need to get to work. The cinema ticket (1.2) is elastic: there are other ways to spend an evening.

## PED and total revenue

Total revenue = price × quantity. PED tells you, before you calculate, which way revenue will move.

| | Before | After | Change in revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bus (inelastic) | $2.00 × 1,000 = $2,000 | $2.50 × 900 = $2,250 | Up $250 |
| Cinema (elastic) | $8 × 500 = $4,000 | $10 × 350 = $3,500 | Down $500 |

The rule:

- **Inelastic demand:** a price rise increases total revenue, and a price cut reduces it.
- **Elastic demand:** a price rise reduces total revenue, and a price cut increases it.
- **Unitary:** total revenue stays the same.

<Tip>
In a written answer, do not stop at "demand is inelastic". Say what it means for the firm in the question: "Because demand is inelastic, the 25% fare rise will increase the bus company's revenue, since passengers fall by only 10%." That last clause is what turns a knowledge mark into an analysis mark.
</Tip>

## What makes demand elastic or inelastic

| Demand tends to be more elastic when | Demand tends to be more inelastic when |
|---|---|
| There are close substitutes (one brand of cola) | There are few substitutes (petrol, electricity) |
| It takes a large share of income (a car, a holiday) | It takes a small share of income (salt, matches) |
| It is a luxury | It is a necessity |
| Buyers have time to adjust | The change is short term |
| | It is habit-forming (cigarettes) |

Use these as reasons in an explain question. "Demand for petrol is price inelastic because there are few substitutes for drivers in the short term" is a full, developed reason. "Petrol is inelastic" is not.

## Why it matters

- **To a firm setting prices.** A firm facing inelastic demand can raise its price and earn more. A firm facing elastic demand may do better by cutting it.
- **To a government choosing what to tax.** Taxes on goods with inelastic demand, such as petrol and cigarettes, raise a lot of revenue because consumption falls only a little. If the aim is to cut consumption, the same inelastic demand means the tax will not cut it by much.

That second point is a classic "discuss" question. The two aims pull against each other, and the PED tells you which one the tax is better at.

## Quick check

The price of a phone case falls from $20 to $15. Sales rise from 400 to 600 a week. Calculate PED, say whether demand is elastic or inelastic, and say what happened to total revenue.

**Answer.** Price: −5 ÷ 20 × 100 = −25%. Quantity: +200 ÷ 400 × 100 = +50%. PED = 50 ÷ −25 = **−2**, so demand is **price elastic**. Revenue rises from $20 × 400 = $8,000 to $15 × 600 = $9,000, up $1,000, as expected when price is cut and demand is elastic.
