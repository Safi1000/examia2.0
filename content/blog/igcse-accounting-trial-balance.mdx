If your trial balance never balances, you are not bad at Accounting. You are usually making one of three small mistakes, and all three are fixable in an afternoon. This is the method I use with my own students, with a full example you can copy.

## What a trial balance actually is

A trial balance is a list of every ledger account balance on one date, split into two columns: debit and credit. If the double entry has been done correctly, the two columns add up to the same total.

That is all it is. It is not a financial statement, and it does not show profit. It is a check, done before you prepare the income statement and the statement of financial position.

Two things examiners expect you to know straight away:

- It is always prepared **at a date** ("Trial balance at 31 March 2026"), never "for the year".
- Agreeing totals do **not** prove the books are correct. More on that below, because it is a favourite question.

## The one rule: where each balance goes

Every balance belongs on one side. You can learn the whole thing with one word pair: **DEAD CLIC**.

| Debit balances (DEAD) | Credit balances (CLIC) |
|---|---|
| **D**ebit side | **C**redit side |
| **E**xpenses (wages, rent, electricity) | **L**iabilities (trade payables, loans) |
| **A**ssets (premises, equipment, inventory, bank, trade receivables) | **I**ncome (revenue, rent received, discount received) |
| **D**rawings | **C**apital |

If you can say which group an item belongs to, you know its side. That alone fixes most trial balances that refuse to agree.

### The items students put on the wrong side

These are the ones students most often put on the wrong side:

- **Returns inwards** (sales returns): debit. They reduce your revenue, so they sit against it.
- **Returns outwards** (purchases returns): credit. They reduce your purchases.
- **Carriage inwards and carriage outwards**: both debit. Both are expenses.
- **Discount allowed**: debit (an expense). **Discount received**: credit (income).
- **Provision for depreciation** and **provision for doubtful debts**: credit, even though they relate to assets.
- **Bank overdraft**: credit. It is money you owe, so it is a liability.
- **Opening inventory** appears in the trial balance. **Closing inventory** usually does not; it is given as a note underneath.

## Worked example

Sana runs a small shop. Her ledger balances on 31 March 2026 are:

- Premises $60 000, equipment $15 000, inventory at 1 April 2025 $8 000
- Trade receivables $6 500, bank $4 200

## The method, step by step

1. **Write the heading** with the business name and the date: "Trial balance at …".
2. **List every balance** once. Do not leave any account out, including small ones like discounts.
3. **Place each balance** using DEAD CLIC. Do the awkward items above slowly.
4. **Add each column** and write the totals on the same line, double underlined.
5. **If they don't agree**, check in this order: a balance on the wrong side, a balance missed out, then an addition error.

<Tip>
Lay the trial balance out as a neat table with ruled columns, even in rough work. A lot of lost marks here come from untidy columns, where a figure drifts into the wrong side and the totals no longer agree.
</Tip>

## The six errors a trial balance will not show

Examiners love asking about this. In each of these errors the debit and the credit are still equal, so the totals agree even though the books are wrong:

1. **Omission**: a transaction is left out completely.
2. **Commission**: the right amount goes to the right type of account, but the wrong person's account (for example, Ali's account instead of Ahmed's).
3. **Principle**: an item goes to the wrong type of account, such as buying equipment recorded as purchases.
4. **Original entry**: the wrong amount is used on both sides, for example $540 written as $450.
5. **Complete reversal**: the right accounts and amount, but debit and credit swapped.
6. **Compensating errors**: two separate mistakes of the same amount that cancel each other out.

A useful way to remember them: if both sides of the entry were affected in the same way, the trial balance cannot see it.

## When it doesn't balance: the suspense account

If you cannot find the difference straight away, the difference goes into a **suspense account** so the trial balance totals agree for now. When you find each error, you correct it with a journal entry and the suspense account clears to zero. That is a topic of its own, and it comes up often in the structured questions.

## Quick recap

- A trial balance lists ledger balances **at a date** in two columns.
- **DEAD CLIC** tells you the side for every balance.
- Watch returns, carriage, discounts, provisions and overdrafts.
- Agreeing totals do not prove the books are right: learn the six errors.
