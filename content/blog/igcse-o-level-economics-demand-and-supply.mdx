Demand is how much buyers are willing and able to buy at each price. Supply is how much sellers are willing and able to sell. Where the two curves cross, you get the equilibrium price and quantity. A change in the good's own price moves you along a curve. Anything else shifts a curve, and that is what changes the market price.

## What is demand, and why does the demand curve slope down?

Demand is the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a period of time. "Willing" is not enough. A student who wants a new phone but cannot pay for it is not part of demand.

**Market demand** is the total of every buyer's individual demand. If three households buy 2, 3 and 5 kg of mangoes a week at $3, market demand at $3 is 10 kg.

A **demand schedule** is a table of prices and the quantity demanded at each one:

| Price per kg | Quantity demanded (kg a week) |
|---|---|
| $1 | 1,000 |
| $2 | 800 |
| $3 | 600 |
| $4 | 400 |
| $5 | 200 |

Plot it with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis and you get a line that slopes down from left to right. As the price falls, people buy more: the good is cheaper compared with other things, and more people can afford it.

## What is supply, and why does the supply curve slope up?

Supply is the quantity of a good that sellers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a period of time. **Market supply** is the total of every firm's individual supply.

| Price per kg | Quantity supplied (kg a week) |
|---|---|
| $1 | 200 |
| $2 | 400 |
| $3 | 600 |
| $4 | 800 |
| $5 | 1,000 |

The supply curve slopes up from left to right. A higher price makes selling more profitable, so existing farmers sell more and new ones are drawn in.

## What is the difference between a movement along a curve and a shift?

This is the idea the whole topic rests on, and the 0455 and 2281 syllabuses use exact words for it. They ask for the causes of each change but do not list them, so the causes in the two tables below are the standard ones I teach.

| What changes | What happens on the diagram | Words to use |
|---|---|---|
| The price of the good itself | A movement along the existing curve | Demand **extends** or **contracts**; supply **extends** or **contracts** |
| Anything else that affects buyers | The demand curve shifts | Demand **increases** (right) or **decreases** (left) |
| Anything else that affects sellers | The supply curve shifts | Supply **increases** (right) or **decreases** (left) |

So "the price of mangoes rose, so demand for mangoes fell" is wrong. The price rise causes a **contraction** in demand, a movement up the same curve. Demand itself, the whole curve, has not changed.

### What shifts the demand curve?

| Cause | Increase in demand (curve shifts right) | Decrease in demand (curve shifts left) |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Incomes rise (for most goods) | Incomes fall |
| Price of a substitute | Tea gets dearer, so demand for coffee rises | Tea gets cheaper |
| Price of a complement | Printers get cheaper, so demand for ink rises | Printers get dearer |
| Tastes and fashion | A health report praises the good | The good goes out of fashion |
| Advertising | A successful campaign | A rival's campaign draws buyers away |
| Population | More people, or more people in the age group that buys it | Fewer people |

Substitutes are goods used instead of each other (tea and coffee). Complements are goods used together (printers and ink).

### What shifts the supply curve?

| Cause | Increase in supply (curve shifts right) | Decrease in supply (curve shifts left) |
|---|---|---|
| Costs of production | Wages or raw material prices fall | Wages or raw material prices rise |
| Technology | Better machines raise output per worker | Equipment breaks down or wears out |
| Government | A subsidy to producers | A tax on producers |
| Weather and nature (farm goods) | A good harvest | A drought, flood or disease |
| Number of firms | New firms enter the market | Firms leave the market |

A tax on a good adds to the seller's costs, so it shifts **supply** to the left. Buyers then buy less because the price has risen. That fall is a contraction along the demand curve, not a shift of demand.

## How is the equilibrium price set?

Put the two schedules side by side:

| Price per kg | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1 | 1,000 | 200 | Shortage of 800 |
| $2 | 800 | 400 | Shortage of 400 |
| $3 | 600 | 600 | **Equilibrium** |
| $4 | 400 | 800 | Surplus of 400 |
| $5 | 200 | 1,000 | Surplus of 800 |

**Market equilibrium** is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no pressure for the price to change. Here it is $3, with 600 kg traded.

Any other price is **disequilibrium**:

- **Below equilibrium (a shortage).** At $2, buyers want 800 kg but only 400 kg is offered. Buyers compete for the mangoes and sellers see they can charge more, so the price rises. As it rises, demand contracts and supply extends until they meet at $3.
- **Above equilibrium (a surplus).** At $4, 800 kg is offered but buyers want only 400 kg. Unsold stock builds up, sellers cut the price, demand extends and supply contracts until they meet at $3.

This is the **price mechanism**. I teach it as three jobs. Price changes signal to firms what to produce. They give an incentive to produce it in the cheapest way. They ration the good to those willing and able to pay. That is how a market answers the what, how and for whom questions without anyone planning it.

## How do you draw a demand and supply diagram for the exam?

I teach the same eight steps every time, in the same order, so nothing is forgotten under time pressure.

1. Draw two axes. Label the vertical axis **Price** and the horizontal axis **Quantity**. Mark the origin O.
2. Draw a straight line sloping down from left to right. Label it **D1**.
3. Draw a straight line sloping up from left to right, crossing D1 near the middle. Label it **S1**.
4. From the crossing point, draw a dotted line across to the price axis and label it **P1**. Draw a dotted line down to the quantity axis and label it **Q1**.
5. Decide which curve the event affects. Buyers: demand. Sellers or costs: supply.
6. Decide the direction. More wanted or more offered at every price: right. Less: left.
7. Draw the new curve parallel to the old one and label it **D2** or **S2**. An arrow between the two makes the shift clear.
8. Mark the new crossing point with dotted lines to **P2** and **Q2**.

Use a ruler and a pencil, and draw it big: about half a page. A small, crowded diagram is where labels get lost.

<Tip title="Check four things before you move on">
Both axes, both original curves, the new curve, and both equilibriums. Examiner reports describe candidates who understood the economics and still lost marks through missing labels.
</Tip>

## What happens to price and quantity when demand or supply changes?

There are only four single shifts. Learn the result of each one.

| Change | Curve moves | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase in demand | D shifts right | Rises | Rises |
| Decrease in demand | D shifts left | Falls | Falls |
| Increase in supply | S shifts right | Falls | Rises |
| Decrease in supply | S shifts left | Rises | Falls |

Read the table this way: a demand shift moves price and quantity in the **same** direction; a supply shift moves them in **opposite** directions.

Each shift also causes a movement along the other curve. When demand increases, the price rises, so supply **extends**. When supply decreases, the price rises, so demand **contracts**. Strong written answers name both.

If both curves shift at once, one of the two results depends on which shift is bigger. For example, if demand and supply both increase, quantity certainly rises, but price could rise, fall or stay the same. Only draw both shifts when the question gives you two events.

## Worked example: what happens to the price of mangoes when incomes rise?

Start with the mango market above: equilibrium at $3 and 600 kg a week. Incomes in the town rise, and at every price buyers now want 400 kg more.

| Price per kg | Old demand | New demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1 | 1,000 | 1,400 | 200 |
| $2 | 800 | 1,200 | 400 |
| $3 | 600 | 1,000 | 600 |
| $4 | 400 | 800 | 800 |
| $5 | 200 | 600 | 1,000 |

**Step 1. Which curve, which way?** Income affects buyers, and buyers want more at every price. Demand increases: D1 shifts right to D2.

**Step 2. What happens at the old price?** At $3, buyers now want 1,000 kg but sellers still offer 600 kg. There is a shortage of 1,000 minus 600 = **400 kg**.

**Step 3. How does the market adjust?** The shortage pushes the price up. As it rises, supply extends (sellers offer more) and the new demand contracts (buyers want a little less than 1,000 kg).

**Step 4. Where is the new equilibrium?** At $4, quantity demanded is 800 and quantity supplied is 800. The new equilibrium is **$4 and 800 kg**.

**Step 5. What happened to sellers' revenue?**

| | Price | Quantity | Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | $3 | 600 kg | $1,800 |
| After | $4 | 800 kg | $3,200 |

Revenue rose by $3,200 minus $1,800 = **$1,400** a week.

**The diagram in words.** Axes Price and Quantity. D1 and S1 cross at P1 ($3) and Q1 (600). D2 sits to the right of D1. D2 and S1 cross at P2 ($4) and Q2 (800). Price and quantity have both risen, as the table of four shifts predicts.

Notice what did **not** happen: the supply curve did not move. Sellers sold more because the price rose, which is an extension along S1. If you shift supply as well, you have drawn a different event.

## How are 4 and 6 mark diagram questions marked?

From 2027, Paper 2 (2 hours, 80 marks, 70% of the qualification) has Question 1 in Section A, which you must answer, and three questions from four in Section B. The structure is the same in 0455 and 2281.

### The 4 mark "Draw" question

In the 2027 specimen paper, Question 1(d) asks you to draw a demand and supply diagram for a change in one market. The mark scheme splits the 4 marks like this:

| Mark | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both axes labelled (price and quantity) |
| 1 | Original demand and supply curves labelled (D1, S1) |
| 1 | The new curve shifted in the right direction (D2 or S2) |
| 1 | Both equilibriums shown: P1, Q1 and P2, Q2, or points E1 and E2 |

The same scheme says no marks are given for written analysis. When the command word is "Draw", draw. Examiner reports from 2023, 2025 and 2026 all describe candidates writing explanations that the question did not ask for.

### The 6 mark "Analyse" question

Section B part (c) questions are worth 6 marks and use the command word "Analyse". In the 2027 specimen, the one on this topic asks how maximum and minimum prices cause disequilibrium. It gives 1 mark for showing or stating where each price is set (2 marks), and the other 4 for a chain of analysis: the price changes, demand extends or contracts, supply extends or contracts, and a shortage or surplus results.

So in a 6 mark analyse question, a diagram can earn the first marks, but most of the marks are for the written chain. Write each link as its own step: "Costs rise, so supply decreases, so the price rises, so demand contracts and fewer units are sold."

### What older past papers look like

Some older papers, such as June and November 2024, ask you to analyse using a demand and supply diagram in Section A for 5 marks. Their mark schemes give the same 4 diagram marks plus 1 mark for a written link, such as higher costs leading to a higher price. They are still excellent practice. Find them under [demand](/past-papers/igcse-economics-0455/topics/2-2-demand) and [price changes](/past-papers/igcse-economics-0455/topics/2-5-price-changes) on the 0455 topic pages, and see how often this area comes up on the [most repeated topics](/past-papers/igcse-economics-0455/most-repeated-topics) page.

## Where do students lose marks on demand and supply diagrams?

Every row below comes from a Cambridge examiner report for 0455 or 2281.

| Mistake | What to do instead | Report |
|---|---|---|
| Demand and supply curves the wrong way round | Demand slopes down, supply slopes up. Label each one | 0455 June 2023 and June 2025; 2281 November 2023 |
| Axes labelled "demand" and "supply", "income", or "capital goods" | Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis | 0455 June 2022 |
| Shifting the wrong curve | Ask: does this affect buyers or sellers? | 0455 June 2022, June 2025, March 2026 |
| Shifting the right curve the wrong way | Ask: more or less at every price? | 0455 March 2023, June 2025 |
| No original or new equilibrium shown | Dotted lines to P1, Q1 and P2, Q2, every time | 0455 June 2025 (the most common error), November 2022 |
| Two separate diagrams, one for demand and one for supply | One diagram with both curves on it | 0455 June 2025 |
| A full explanation under a "Draw" question | Draw only; save the time for later questions | 0455 March 2023, June 2023, June 2025; 2281 November 2023 |
| Describing the diagram instead of the market | Name the market: "demand for tea increases", not "the curve shifts" | 0455 November 2022 |

The June 2025 report was blunt about the two diagram approach: "This approach cannot gain full marks." And in a November 2022 multiple choice question about a tax, only 40 per cent chose the right answer; many shifted demand when buyers were only reacting to the higher price.

## How does a price change affect sales?

Every shift changes sales, but by how much depends on how buyers and sellers respond to price.

- When **supply decreases**, the price rises and sales fall. If demand is price inelastic, sales fall only a little and sellers' revenue rises. If demand is price elastic, sales fall a lot and revenue falls.
- When **demand increases**, both price and sales rise, so revenue rises.

This is where demand and supply meets price elasticity. My [price elasticity of demand guide](/blog/igcse-economics-price-elasticity-of-demand) shows how to work it out and how it changes revenue. Demand and supply are also the starting point for [market failure](/blog/igcse-economics-market-failure), though the 2027 syllabus says demand and supply diagrams for market failure are not required.

## Can you answer these practice questions?

**1.** For the market for tea, say whether each event causes a movement along a curve or a shift, and name the curve.

(a) The price of tea rises. (b) The price of coffee rises. (c) Wages on tea plantations rise. (d) A government subsidy is paid to tea growers.

**Answer.** (a) A movement: demand for tea contracts and supply extends. (b) A shift: coffee is a substitute, so demand for tea increases (shifts right). (c) A shift: costs rise, so supply decreases (shifts left). (d) A shift: supply increases (shifts right).

**2.** In the mango market, a drought cuts supply. Use the table to find the old and new equilibrium and the change in sellers' revenue.

| Price per kg | Quantity demanded | Old supply | New supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| $2 | 800 | 400 | 0 |
| $3 | 600 | 600 | 200 |
| $4 | 400 | 800 | 400 |
| $5 | 200 | 1,000 | 600 |

**Answer.** Old equilibrium: $3 and 600 kg, where 600 demanded equals 600 supplied. New equilibrium: $4 and 400 kg, where 400 demanded equals 400 supplied. Revenue falls from $3 × 600 = $1,800 to $4 × 400 = $1,600, down **$200**. Price rose by a third and sales fell by a third, and the lost sales outweigh the higher price.

**3.** Draw a demand and supply diagram to show the effect of a fall in the price of fertiliser on the market for wheat. (4 marks)

**Answer.** Fertiliser is a cost for wheat farmers, so supply increases. Your diagram needs: axes labelled Price and Quantity (1); D1 and S1 labelled (1); S2 drawn to the right of S1 (1); P1, Q1 and P2, Q2 marked, with P2 lower than P1 and Q2 greater than Q1 (1). No writing is needed.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a change in demand and a change in quantity demanded?

A change in quantity demanded is caused by the good's own price and is a movement along the curve (an extension or contraction). A change in demand is caused by anything else, such as income or the price of a substitute, and shifts the whole curve.

### Which axis does price go on?

Price always goes on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Examiner reports list swapped or wrongly named axes as a common way to lose the first mark.

### Do I need to explain my diagram?

Not when the question says "Draw". The 2027 specimen mark scheme gives no marks for written analysis on that question. When the question says "Analyse", you do need written steps, and most of the marks are for them.

### Should I use D1 and D2 or just D?

Use D1 and S1 for the original curves and D2 or S2 for the new one, with P1, Q1, P2 and Q2 for the equilibriums. That is the labelling the mark schemes describe, and it makes the shift impossible to miss.

### Why does a tax shift supply and not demand?

A tax on a good is paid by the seller, so it raises their costs and they offer less at every price. Buyers then buy less because the price is higher, which is a contraction along the demand curve.

### Is demand and supply in both 0455 and 2281?

Yes. Topics 2.2 to 2.5 (demand, supply, price determination and price changes) have the same content in the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029. You can tick off each point on the [0455 syllabus checklist](/syllabus/igcse-economics-0455) or the [2281 syllabus checklist](/syllabus/o-level-economics-2281).
