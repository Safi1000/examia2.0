Yes, IGCSE Economics 0455 changes for exams from 2027. Paper 1 grows to 40 multiple choice questions in 1 hour. Paper 2 shrinks to 80 marks in 2 hours, with a smaller Section A. Evaluation is now only 10% of your grade. Some topics are gone (deflation, fixed exchange rates, population pyramids) and some are new (globalisation, scarcity, seasonal unemployment, sustainability).

The changes are real but not a rewrite. The two-paper format is the same, Cambridge says the new objectives "test most of the same knowledge and skills", and it says the marking criteria "stay largely the same". Below is every change Cambridge lists, what each one means for you, and how I would plan your revision around them.

Everything here comes from Cambridge's official 0455 syllabus for 2027, 2028 and 2029. Where something is not in that document, I say so.

## Who the new syllabus affects

Cambridge says the 0455 syllabus "has been reviewed and revised for first examination in 2027". It is used for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Exams run in the June and November series, and also in March for schools in India.

- **Sitting in June or November 2027 or later:** you are on the new syllabus. This includes resits. Cambridge's rule is to use the syllabus for the year you take the exam, not the year you started the course.
- **Sitting in November 2026:** you are still on the old syllabus. The changes in this article do not apply to you.
- **Starting the course now and sitting in 2028:** you are on the new syllabus from day one. Use it as your checklist from the first lesson.
- **Private candidates:** Cambridge says private candidates can enter for 0455. Your exams centre makes the entry, so ask them early.

A few rules that stay the same but are worth knowing:

- There is no Core and Extended split. Everyone sits the same two papers, and grades run from A\* to G.
- You cannot sit 0455 in the same exam series as O Level Economics 2281 or IGCSE (9-1) Economics 0987. Cambridge treats them as the same level.
- You can retake the whole qualification as many times as you want.
- The syllabus and papers are in English only.
- Cambridge designs the course around roughly 130 guided learning hours. That is a guide, not a rule.

### IGCSE 0455 or O Level 2281?

The two are sister syllabuses. They have the same topic list, the same paper structure for 2027 and the same changes. If you have read my article on [O Level Economics 2281 for 2027](/blog/o-level-economics-2281-syllabus-2027-changes), most of this will look familiar. The main differences are the grade scale (0455 runs A\* to G, 2281 runs A\* to E) and availability. Cambridge has said that from 2028, O Level 2281 is offered in administrative zone 4 only, and it suggested that schools in other zones may wish to consider IGCSE 0455 instead. That 2281 article has the details. If your school is moving from 2281 to 0455, your preparation barely changes: the content you need is the same.

## The papers: old and new side by side

Cambridge's change list touches both papers. Here is everything it says, set against the old version.

| | Before 2027 | 2027 to 2029 |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper 1** Multiple Choice | 30 questions, 30 marks | 40 questions, 40 marks |
| Paper 1 time | 45 minutes | 1 hour |
| **Paper 2** Structured Questions | 90 marks | 80 marks |
| Paper 2 time | 2 hours 15 minutes | 2 hours |
| Section A (Question 1) | More marks than now | 20 marks, parts (a) to (f) |
| Paper 2 mark scheme | Old layout and wording | New layout and wording, marking criteria largely the same |

Cambridge says only that Question 1 "has reduced in marks". If you want the exact old split between Section A and Section B, check the old syllabus or an old paper. I have not listed a number I cannot confirm from the 2027 document.

### The full 2027 paper structure

| | Paper 1 Multiple Choice | Paper 2 Structured Questions |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | 1 hour | 2 hours |
| Marks | 40 | 80 |
| Weighting | 30% | 70% |
| What you answer | All 40 multiple choice questions | Section A: Question 1, compulsory. Section B: three questions from Questions 2 to 5 |
| Question types | Multiple choice, which may include calculations and diagrams | Section A: answers based on unseen information about a real economic situation (text and data, possibly diagrams and tables), parts (a) to (f). Section B: four parts, (a) to (d), each question 20 marks, introduced by a short paragraph |
| Calculator | Allowed | Allowed in both sections |
| Content covered | All of the subject content | All of the subject content |
| Objectives tested | AO1 and AO2 | AO1, AO2 and AO3 |

Two details from the syllabus are easy to miss:

- **Section A must use the data.** Cambridge says candidates "must make use of this information" in their Section A answers. An answer that ignores the extract is throwing marks away.
- **Section B is different.** You may use the short paragraph, or other examples you have studied. Some optional questions may ask you to draw a diagram. Part (d) of each Section B question is marked with levels of response.

### What the numbers mean in practice

The pace does not change. The old Paper 1 gave you 90 seconds per question, and so does the new one (60 minutes for 40 questions). In Paper 2, both versions give you about a minute and a half per mark.

A simple time plan for the new Paper 2:

| Part | Marks | Suggested time |
|---|---:|---:|
| Section A, Question 1 | 20 | about 30 minutes |
| Section B, first question | 20 | about 30 minutes |
| Section B, second question | 20 | about 30 minutes |
| Section B, third question | 20 | about 30 minutes |

That leaves no spare time, so read the four Section B questions once at the start and choose quickly. Section B is now three quarters of Paper 2, so your choice of questions matters more than it used to.

On weightings: one Paper 1 mark is worth 0.75% of your final grade (30% over 40 marks). One Paper 2 mark is worth 0.875% (70% over 80 marks). A Paper 2 mark counts for more than a multiple choice mark, so do not rush Paper 2 to save time for anything else.

## The assessment objectives: less evaluation

This is the change most students miss. Cambridge has reworded the assessment objectives and changed their weightings. It says the weighting of AO3 Evaluation "has decreased", and the weightings of AO1 and AO2 "have increased".

| Assessment objective | What it asks you to do | Whole qualification | Paper 1 | Paper 2 |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| AO1 Knowledge and understanding | Show knowledge of definitions, formulas, concepts and theories. Use economic terminology. | 43% | 50% | 40% |
| AO2 Analysis | Select, organise and interpret data. Apply analysis to written, numerical, diagrammatic and graphical data. Develop links and relationships. | 47% | 50% | 45% |
| AO3 Evaluation | Evaluate information, data and arguments. Recognise that economic decisions have uncertain outcomes. | 10% | 0% | 15% |

Cambridge also says the reworded objectives "test most of the same knowledge and skills as in the previous syllabus". So the skills are the same. The balance has moved.

What this means for your answers:

- **Exact definitions matter more.** Knowledge is 43% of the qualification. A loose definition of opportunity cost or of a recession now costs a bigger share of your grade.
- **Analysis is the biggest objective.** Almost half the qualification rewards you for explaining how one thing leads to another, with data and diagrams used correctly.
- **Evaluation is focused on part (d).** The syllabus says part (d) questions in Section B need "reasoned discussions that consider two sides of economic arguments". Save your "on the other hand" and your judgement for those parts. When a question only says explain, explain.

<Tip>
For every "explain" or "analyse" question, write a chain: the cause, how it works, the effect, then the effect on the group the question names. A chain with three or four clear links is analysis. A list of separate points is mostly knowledge, and earns less.
</Tip>

The command words in the 2027 syllabus are analyse, calculate, define, describe, discuss, explain, give, identify and state. "Discuss" means writing about an issue in depth in a structured way, which in practice is where your two-sided answers go.

## Content removed: the full list

Cambridge lists these as removed or significantly reduced. Topic numbers are from the **old** syllabus.

| What was removed | What stays | What this means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Old 3.4 Trade unions as a separate topic | Trade unions and their bargaining power, inside new 3.3.2 Wage determination | Learn unions as part of wages, not as a stand-alone topic. |
| Old 3.5.3 Causes and forms of the growth of firms | Mergers (new 3.4.2) and small and large firms (new 3.4.1) | Old questions on internal and external growth in general are off-syllabus. Mergers are still in. |
| Old 4.1 The role of government | Government macroeconomic aims, new 4.1 | The new 4.1 is about aims and conflicts between them. |
| Old 4.3.5 Principles of taxation | Reasons for taxation and tax classifications, new 4.2.3 | Stop drilling the principles of a good tax. Learn the six reasons for taxation instead. |
| Old 4.7.2 Changing patterns and level of employment | Definitions, measurement, types and consequences of unemployment, new 4.6 | Old questions about shifts in employment patterns are off-syllabus. |
| Old 4.8 content about deflation | The definition of deflation, new 4.7.1 | Know what deflation means. Its wider causes and consequences have gone. |
| Old 5.3.3 Interpreting population pyramids | Effects of changes in size and structure of population, new 5.3.2 | You still need age and gender structure, but not pyramid reading. |
| Old 6.3.5 Fixed foreign exchange rates | Floating exchange rates, new 6.3.3 | Fixed systems, and the comparison of fixed and floating, are gone. |
| Mobility of capital, enterprise and land | Mobility of labour, new 3.3.4 | Only labour mobility is examined now. |
| "Between different households and over time", under household spending | The influences on spending, saving and borrowing, new 3.2.1 | The list of influences stays and grows (see below). |
| PPC references under recession | Causes and consequences of recession, new 4.5.4 | Explain recession through total demand and resources, not a PPC. |
| PPC references, and "changes in investment and technology", under causes of economic growth | Growth caused by total demand, or the quantity or quality of resources, new 4.5.3 | Use the three causes Cambridge now lists. PPC diagrams still appear in topic 1.4. |
| The claimant count, under measurement of unemployment | The labour force survey and the unemployment rate formula, new 4.6.2 | Learn the labour force survey method only. |

### Old past paper questions that are now off-syllabus

Past papers are still the best practice you have. Based on Cambridge's list, skip or adapt old questions that ask you to:

- explain or compare fixed and floating exchange rate systems
- discuss the causes or consequences of deflation (a one-line definition is still fine)
- read or interpret a population pyramid
- explain the claimant count method of measuring unemployment
- discuss the principles or canons of taxation
- explain the causes and forms of growth of firms in general (merger questions are still in)
- explain changing patterns of employment over time
- discuss the mobility of capital, land or enterprise
- use a PPC to show economic growth or recession inside the growth topic

If you are unsure about a question, check it against the 2027 topic list. If the idea is not in the new syllabus, do not spend revision time on it.

## Content added: the full list

These are now examinable, or spelled out more clearly than before. Topic numbers are from the **new** syllabus.

| What was added | Where | What this means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Environment and sustainability, built into topics throughout | Several topics (see the next section) | Expect environmental contexts in data and questions. |
| The concept of scarcity | 1.1.1 | A definition you must know exactly. It sits next to the basic economic problem. |
| The terms perfectly inelastic, unitary elastic and perfectly elastic | 2.6.2 (PED) and 2.7.2 (PES) | Know each value, what it means, and the shape of its curve. |
| Monopoly as a term associated with market failure | 2.9.2 | Abuse of monopoly power is listed as a cause of market failure. |
| Non-provision of public goods, and higher prices under monopoly, as results of misallocation | 2.9.4 | Two more consequences of market failure to explain. |
| Arguments for and against the mixed economic system | 2.10.2 | A ready-made part (d) question. Learn both sides. |
| Age and culture as influences on household finances | 3.2.1 | The list is now income, rate of interest, confidence, age and culture. |
| Drawing and interpreting national minimum wage diagrams | 3.3.2 | A new diagram to practise in the labour market. |
| Definitions of the types of mergers | 3.4.2 | Horizontal, vertical and conglomerate, with examples. |
| Average total cost diagrams for economies and diseconomies of scale | 3.4.3 | A new diagram. Practise drawing and labelling it. |
| Effects of changes in investment on productivity | 3.5.3 | Link investment to productivity in your chains of analysis. |
| Characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of competitive markets | 3.7.1 | No diagrams needed, but you must explain effects on price, quality, choice and profit. |
| Effect of having only one firm on price, quality, choice and profit | 3.7.2 | The monopoly side of the same comparison. No diagrams needed. |

| Infrastructure spending as a supply-side policy | 4.4.2 | Add it to your supply-side list. |
| Causes and consequences of recession | 4.5.4 | A new subtopic with its own definition. |
| Seasonal unemployment, covered explicitly | 4.6.3 | The four types are now frictional, structural, cyclical and seasonal. |
| Improved healthcare provision as a policy against poverty | 5.2.3 | Add it next to education, benefits, progressive tax and the NMW. |
| Causes and consequences of changes in globalisation | 6.2.2 | A whole new subtopic (details below). |
| Definitions of specialisation by country and of free trade | 6.1.1 and 6.1.2 | Two definitions to learn word-perfect. |
| Reasons for trade restrictions, and advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade | 6.2.5 and 6.2.6 | Eight reasons to learn, and both sides for part (d). |
| Reasons for buying and selling foreign currencies | 6.3.2 | Six reasons to learn (listed below). |
| Definitions of floating exchange rate, appreciation and depreciation | 6.3.3 | Learn them exactly, since fixed rates have gone. |

### Where sustainability now appears

Cambridge says environment and sustainability references are "integrated throughout the content". In the 2027 syllabus, that includes:

- **4.1 Macroeconomic aims:** environmental sustainability is now one of the government's aims. "Economic growth and environmental sustainability" is listed as a possible conflict between aims.
- **4.2.3 Taxation:** encouraging environmental sustainability is one of the reasons for taxation.
- **5.2.2 Poverty:** environmental factors are listed as a cause of poverty.
- **6.2.2 Globalisation:** you need the effects of changes in globalisation on the environment.
- **6.2.5 Trade restrictions:** promoting environmental sustainability is one of the reasons for restricting trade.
- **2.10.3 Government intervention:** quotas are listed with an example, the extraction of natural resources.

What this means for you: the economics underneath does not change. It is still costs and benefits, trade-offs, and who gains and who loses. But the contexts in Section A and in Section B stems may well be environmental, so practise applying your theory to them.

### The new globalisation topic

Topic 6.2.2 is new and gives you a clear answer structure.

- **Causes of changes in globalisation:** changes in trade restrictions, changes in transport costs, changes in communication costs, and the movement of multinational companies.
- **Effects you need to explain:** on international trade, competition, the environment, migration, income distribution and economic development.

Learn the four causes and six effects as a grid. Most questions will ask you to link one cause to one or two effects.

### Three lists worth learning word for word

These lists are short, now explicit, and easy marks.

| Topic | The list in the 2027 syllabus |
|---|---|
| Reasons for taxation (4.2.3) | Raising revenue, discouraging consumption of demerit goods, reducing imports, redistributing income, influencing total demand, encouraging environmental sustainability |
| Reasons for trade restrictions (6.2.5) | Protect infant (sunrise) industries, protect declining (sunset) industries, protect strategic industries, avoid dumping, reduce a current account deficit, raise tax revenue, restrict the import of demerit goods, promote environmental sustainability |
| Reasons for buying and selling foreign currencies (6.3.2) | Trade in goods and services, speculation, government intervention in currency markets, payment of profit, interest and dividends between countries, workers' remittances, investment in capital goods between countries |

## Reworded, reordered and renumbered

Cambridge also says:

- "The order of some content has changed and wording has been updated to improve clarity."
- "Some topics have been reorganised and reduced in size."
- An introductory explanation has been added for each topic.
- The syllabus aims have been updated.

What this means for you: topic numbers in old notes and old textbooks may not match. For example, old 3.4 was trade unions, but new 3.4 is Firms. Old 4.1 was the role of government, but new 4.1 is government macroeconomic intervention. Inflation is now 4.7. Always label your notes with the new numbers so your checklist matches the syllabus.

Cambridge says any textbook endorsed for exams from 2027 is suitable for this syllabus. If your textbook is older, use it alongside the removed and added lists above.

The six main topics for 2027 are: the basic economic problem, the allocation of resources, microeconomic decision-makers, government and the macroeconomy, economic development, and international trade and globalisation. You can see every subtopic on my [0455 syllabus page](/syllabus/igcse-economics-0455).

## Diagrams: what you need and what you do not

The 2027 syllabus is clear about diagrams, which helps you plan.

**Needed (drawing and interpretation):** PPC, demand and supply (movements, shifts, equilibrium, disequilibrium), PED and PES curves including the perfect and unitary cases, PED and revenue, maximum and minimum prices, indirect taxes, subsidies, labour market changes, national minimum wage, ATC curves for economies and diseconomies of scale, cost curves showing how output affects costs, and the foreign exchange market.

**Not required:** the syllabus says demand and supply diagrams for market failure are not required, and that diagrams are not required for competitive markets or monopoly markets.

What this means for you: the two new diagrams to practise are the national minimum wage and the ATC curve. Do not waste time on monopoly diagrams. They will not earn you marks at this level.

## Common mistakes with the new syllabus

1. **Revising from old notes without checking them.** Pages on deflation, fixed exchange rates and population pyramids eat time and earn nothing now.
2. **Writing two-sided answers everywhere.** Evaluation is 10% of the qualification. Use it in part (d), and when a question asks you to discuss. Elsewhere, explain clearly and move on.
3. **Spending too long on Section A.** Question 1 is 20 marks now, a quarter of Paper 2. About 30 minutes is right.
4. **Ignoring the Section A data.** Cambridge says you must use it. Quote a figure, then explain it.
5. **Loose definitions.** Knowledge is 43%. Scarcity, free trade, specialisation by country, floating exchange rate, appreciation and depreciation are all new or newly explicit, so learn them exactly.
6. **Forgetting the new diagrams.** Practise the national minimum wage and ATC diagrams until you can draw them from memory.
7. **Old timings in mocks.** Practise Paper 1 at 40 questions in 1 hour, and Paper 2 at 80 marks in 2 hours.
8. **Treating sustainability as a separate topic.** It is built into aims, taxation, poverty, globalisation and trade. Practise it inside those topics.

## A prep plan for a 2027 candidate

Here is how I would plan the year for a student sitting in 2027. Adjust the months if you sit in November. If you sit in 2028, spread the same steps over two years.

### Term 1: now to December

- Print the 2027 topic list and use it as a checklist. Tick each subtopic only when you can define, explain and draw it.
- Go through your old notes with the removed and added tables above. Cross out what has gone. Write new pages for scarcity, globalisation (6.2.2), recession (4.5.4), seasonal unemployment, reasons for taxation, reasons for trade restrictions and reasons for buying and selling currencies.
- Learn the new definitions and the three lists word for word.
- Practise the two new diagrams every week.

### Term 2: January to March

- Start timed past paper practice. Use [IGCSE Economics 0455 past papers](/past-papers/igcse-economics-0455), but skip questions on removed content (see the list above).
- For Paper 1, do sets of 40 questions in 1 hour. Mark them, and keep a list of topics you get wrong.
- For Paper 2, practise one Section A question in 30 minutes, then Section B questions in 30 minutes each. When you use an old Section A question, remember the new one is worth 20 marks.
- Practise part (d) answers: two sides, then a clear judgement that depends on something specific.
- Use the updated specimen papers and mark schemes from Cambridge. The syllabus says they show the new format and how answers meet the assessment objectives. Your school can get them from Cambridge's School Support Hub.

### Term 3: April to the exams

- Do full papers under exam conditions, both papers in one sitting if you can.
- Revise your wrong-answer list, not the topics you already know.
- Read the Section A data carefully every time, and use it in every part.
- In the last week, revise definitions, the three lists and your diagrams. Stop learning new material.

## FAQ

**Does IGCSE Economics 0455 change in 2027?**
Yes. Paper 1 becomes 40 questions in 1 hour, Paper 2 becomes 80 marks in 2 hours, evaluation drops to 10% of the qualification, and some content is removed and added. The code stays 0455.

**I am sitting in November 2026. Do I need the new syllabus?**
No. The new syllabus is for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Use the syllabus for the year you sit the exam.

**I am resitting in 2027. Which syllabus do I use?**
The new one. Resits follow the same rule: the syllabus for the year you sit.

**Are old past papers still useful?**
Yes, very. Most questions still match the 2027 content. Skip questions on removed content, and remember that the old papers had different marks and timings.

**Is the new syllabus harder?**
Not obviously. The pace per mark is the same, and most content carries over. Some topics are gone and some are new. There is less evaluation, but more weight on exact knowledge and clear analysis.

**Is 0455 the same as O Level 2281?**
The topic list and the 2027 changes are the same, and so is the paper structure. The grade scale differs (A\* to G for 0455, A\* to E for 2281), and you cannot sit both in the same series. My [2281 article](/blog/o-level-economics-2281-syllabus-2027-changes) covers the O Level side.

**Is there still a Core and Extended option?**
No. Everyone takes both papers, and grades run from A\* to G.

**Can I still use my old textbook?**
Yes, with care. Cambridge says textbooks endorsed for exams from 2027 are suitable. With an older book, use the removed and added lists above to fill the gaps.

## The bottom line

The 2027 changes to IGCSE Economics 0455 are moderate. The format is familiar, the pace is the same, and most of the content carries over. What changes is the balance: more multiple choice questions, a smaller Section A, less evaluation, and a handful of new topics and diagrams. Update your notes, practise to the new timings, and use old past papers with the off-syllabus list in mind.
