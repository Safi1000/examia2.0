Most students can lay out an appropriation account. The marks slip away on the details around it: a loan from a partner, interest on drawings that has to be worked out by the month, or a year when the partners are promised more than the business earned. This example has all three, plus a current account that ends the year overdrawn.

If you want the plain layout first, my [Accounting formats article](/blog/igcse-o-level-accounting-formats) has a filled-in appropriation account and columnar current accounts. This article picks up where that one stops.

## What the 0452 syllabus asks for

The Cambridge IGCSE Accounting syllabus expects you to:

- explain the purpose of an appropriation account
- record interest on partners' loans, interest on capital, interest on drawings, partners' salaries and the division of the balance of profit or loss
- explain the uses of, and the differences between, partners' capital accounts and current accounts
- prepare capital and current accounts in ledger form and as part of the statement of financial position

It leaves out a partner joining or leaving, dissolving a partnership and changing the profit-sharing ratio.

## The question

Sana and Omar are in partnership. The year ended 31 December 2025. Their agreement says:

- Interest on capital: 6% a year.
- Omar's salary: $9,000 a year.
- Interest on drawings: 10% a year, from the date of each drawing to the end of the year.
- Profits and losses are shared Sana 2 : Omar 1.

On 1 January 2025, Omar lent the business $10,000 at 5% interest a year. The interest for the year was paid by bank.

| | Sana | Omar |
|---|---|---|
| Capital account (fixed) | $50,000 | $30,000 |
| Current account at 1 January 2025 | $1,400 credit | $700 credit |
| Drawings | $6,000 on 1 July 2025 | $8,000 on 1 October 2025 |

The draft profit, before any interest on the loan, was $12,300.

<BookingBox />

## Step 1: the loan interest is an expense

Interest on a partner's **loan** is not an appropriation. The loan is a debt of the business, the same as a bank loan, so its interest is an expense in the income statement.

Loan interest: 5% × $10,000 = **$500**.

Profit for the year: $12,300 − $500 = **$11,800**. This is the figure that goes into the appropriation account.

If the interest had **not** been paid, it would still be an expense, and the $500 would be credited to Omar's current account instead.

## Step 2: interest on drawings, by the month

When the agreement charges interest from the date of each drawing, count the months to the end of the year:

| Partner | Drawing | Months to 31 December | Interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sana | $6,000 on 1 July | 6 | $6,000 × 10% × 6/12 = **$300** |
| Omar | $8,000 on 1 October | 3 | $8,000 × 10% × 3/12 = **$200** |

Omar took more, but pays less interest, because the money left the business later. Students who charge a full year's interest here lose the mark and the knock-on marks after it.

## Step 3: the appropriation account

**Sana and Omar: appropriation account for the year ended 31 December 2025**

| | $ | $ |
|---|---|---|
| Profit for the year | | 11,800 |
| Add interest on drawings: Sana | 300 | |
| Omar | 200 | 500 |
| | | 12,300 |
| Less salary: Omar | | (9,000) |
| | | 3,300 |
| Less interest on capital: Sana (6% × 50,000) | 3,000 | |
| Omar (6% × 30,000) | 1,800 | (4,800) |
| Residual loss | | (1,500) |
| Share of residual loss: Sana (2/3) | (1,000) | |
| Omar (1/3) | (500) | (1,500) |

The partners were promised $13,800 in salary and interest on capital, but only $12,300 was available. The agreement still applies in full, so the $1,500 shortfall is a **residual loss**, shared in the profit-sharing ratio. Do not stop at $3,300 and share that, and do not reduce the salary to make it fit.

## Step 4: the current accounts

Salary and interest on capital go on the **credit** side, because they are amounts the partner has earned. Drawings, interest on drawings and a share of a loss go on the **debit** side.

**Current accounts**

| Dr | Sana $ | Omar $ | Cr | Sana $ | Omar $ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drawings | 6,000 | 8,000 | Balance b/d | 1,400 | 700 |
| Interest on drawings | 300 | 200 | Salary | | 9,000 |
| Share of residual loss | 1,000 | 500 | Interest on capital | 3,000 | 1,800 |
| Balance c/d | | 2,800 | Balance c/d | 2,900 | |
| | 7,300 | 11,500 | | 7,300 | 11,500 |
| Balance b/d | 2,900 | | Balance b/d | | 2,800 |

Look at Sana's account. The debits come to $7,300 and the credits, before balancing, to $4,400. Sana has taken out $2,900 more than earned, so the balance carried down sits on the credit side, and the balance brought down on 1 January 2026 is a **debit** of $2,900. Omar's account ends with a credit of $2,800.

## Step 5: the statement of financial position

**Extract at 31 December 2025**

| | Sana | Omar | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| | $ | $ | $ |
| Capital accounts | 50,000 | 30,000 | 80,000 |
| Current accounts | (2,900) | 2,800 | (100) |
| | | | 79,900 |
| Non-current liability: loan from Omar | | | 10,000 |

A debit current account is shown in brackets and taken away. Omar's loan is not part of Omar's capital. It goes under liabilities, as non-current if it is not due for repayment within a year.

## Why keep capital and current accounts apart?

This comes up as an explain question, and the example shows the answer. Sana's capital is still $50,000, the amount the partners agreed Sana would invest. The current account tells the separate story of the year: $3,000 of interest on capital came in, but drawings, the interest on them and the share of the loss came to $7,300, so Sana ended the year owing the business $2,900. If everything went through one account, Sana's capital would have dropped to $47,100, and nobody could see at a glance that the agreed investment was still in place.

## Where the marks go in this kind of question

| Slip | What it costs |
|---|---|
| Loan interest put in the appropriation account | The profit for the year is wrong, so every figure after it is wrong too. |
| A full year's interest on drawings | Both interest on drawings figures are wrong, and so is every figure that depends on them. |
| Appropriations cut down to fit the profit | The agreement is not followed, and the residual loss marks are lost. |
| Sana's closing balance written as a credit | The statement of financial position shows $5,800 too much. |
| The loan shown as part of Omar's capital | Capital and liabilities are both misstated. |

<Tip>
Before you draw any account, write one line of working for each figure: the loan interest, both interest on drawings figures, both interest on capital figures, and the residual. If the residual and the two shares agree, the accounts that follow will balance.
</Tip>
