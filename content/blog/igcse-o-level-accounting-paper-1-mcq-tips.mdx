Accounting multiple choice is not a memory test. Most questions are a small calculation or a double entry in disguise, and the four options are the answers you get from doing it right and from the three most common ways of doing it wrong. The habit that saves marks is simple: work the answer out on the question paper first, and only then look at A, B, C and D.

## Paper 1 facts (from the Cambridge syllabuses)

IGCSE 0452 and O Level 7707 have the same Paper 1. The format changes in 2027.

| | Exams in 2026 | Exams from 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Questions | 35, answer all | 40, answer all |
| Time | 1 hour 15 minutes | 1 hour 30 minutes |
| Marks | 35 | 40 |

In 2026, Paper 1 is 30% of the qualification, covers the whole syllabus, and may include calculations. Calculators are allowed. For 7707, the 2026 syllabus weights Paper 1 at 80% knowledge and understanding and 20% analysis, with no evaluation. That explains the style: you are rarely asked for a judgement, but you are constantly asked to apply a rule exactly.

### Your pace

In 2026 you have just over **2 minutes a question** (75 minutes for 35). From 2027 it is **2 minutes 15 seconds** (90 minutes for 40). A definition question takes 30 seconds. The time you save there pays for the control account and depreciation questions.

## Work it out before you look

For every calculation question, write the working in the margin as if it were a Paper 2 question, then match your answer to an option. The wrong options are built from the usual slips, so if you look first, a wrong answer that "seems right" will pull you in.

What the wrong options are usually built from:

- **The opposite side of the entry.** Debit and credit swapped, or an increase treated as a decrease.
- **One step missed.** Depreciation for one year when the question asks for the second, or a receipt counted without the discount allowed.
- **The wrong total.** Cost instead of net realisable value, or the amount paid instead of the amount for the year.

## The question types, and what to write first

| Type | Write this first |
|---|---|
| Accounting equation | Assets = Capital + Liabilities, with the numbers in |
| Double entry | The two accounts, with Dr and Cr next to each |
| Errors | Does the error affect one side only? If not, the trial balance still agrees |
| Accruals and prepayments | Paid, then add owing or take off prepaid, for the year |
| Depreciation | Cost, then each year's charge on its own line |
| Inventory | Cost and net realisable value side by side; take the lower |
| Control accounts | Opening balance, then each item with a + or − |
| Ratios | The formula, then the numbers |

The errors that a trial balance will and will not show are set out in full in my [trial balance article](/blog/igcse-accounting-trial-balance).

## Seven practice questions (my own)

Cover each answer and try the question first.

**Question 1.** A business has assets of $85,000 and liabilities of $23,000. What is its capital?

A $23,000  
B $62,000  
C $85,000  
D $108,000

**Answer: B.** Capital = Assets − Liabilities = $85,000 − $23,000 = $62,000. Option D adds instead of subtracting.

**Question 2.** Which error would cause the trial balance totals to disagree?

A the purchase of a delivery van debited to the motor expenses account  
B a credit sale to Ali entered in Ahmed's account  
C a payment of rent entered in the cash book only  
D a sale left out of the books completely

**Answer: C.** An entry on one side only makes one column bigger than the other. A is an error of principle, B an error of commission and D an error of omission. In each, the debits and credits still match, so the totals agree.

**Question 3.** During the year a business paid rent of $11,000. At the year end, $1,000 of rent was still owing. What is the rent expense in the income statement?

A $10,000  
B $11,000  
C $12,000  
D $13,000

**Answer: C.** The expense is the rent for the year, not the amount paid: $11,000 + $1,000 owing = $12,000. Option A takes the accrual off instead of adding it.

**Question 4.** A machine cost $20,000. It is depreciated at 25% a year using the reducing balance method. What is the depreciation charge for the second year?

A $2,500  
B $3,125  
C $3,750  
D $5,000

**Answer: C.** Year 1: $20,000 × 25% = $5,000, leaving $15,000. Year 2: $15,000 × 25% = $3,750. Option D is year 1, or the straight-line figure on cost.

**Question 5.** An item of inventory cost $400. It can be sold for $450 after repairs costing $80. At what value should it be shown in the statement of financial position?

A $80  
B $370  
C $400  
D $450

**Answer: B.** Net realisable value is $450 − $80 = $370, which is lower than the cost of $400. Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value.

**Question 6.** A business has current assets of $36,000 and current liabilities of $24,000. What is its current ratio?

A 0.67 : 1  
B 1.33 : 1  
C 1.5 : 1  
D 2.5 : 1

**Answer: C.** Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities = $36,000 ÷ $24,000 = 1.5 : 1. Option A divides the wrong way round.

**Question 7.** The trade receivables at the start of the year were $8,000. Credit sales were $40,000, cash received from credit customers was $35,000, discounts allowed were $1,000 and irrecoverable debts written off were $500. What is the closing balance on the sales ledger control account?

A $11,500  
B $12,000  
C $12,500  
D $13,000

**Answer: A.** $8,000 + $40,000 − $35,000 − $1,000 − $500 = $11,500. Option B forgets the irrecoverable debt, and C forgets the discount.

<Tip>
When two options differ by exactly one figure in the question, such as $500 or $1,000 here, the question is testing whether you included that figure. Check it before you choose.
</Tip>

## Marking your practice papers

When you mark a past Paper 1, sort every wrong answer by question type from the table above, not just by topic. After three papers you will usually find that most lost marks sit in two or three types. Those are the ones to drill, with the working written out in full, until they stop going wrong.
