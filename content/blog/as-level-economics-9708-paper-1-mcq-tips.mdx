If you did well in O Level or IGCSE multiple choice, AS Paper 1 can still come as a shock. The basics are the same: four options, one mark each, no penalty for a wrong answer. What changes is the number of steps between reading the question and reaching the answer. Reading NOT questions, pacing and the answer sheet are covered in my [O Level and IGCSE Paper 1 article](/blog/o-level-igcse-economics-paper-1-mcq-tips).

## Paper 1 facts (from the 9708 syllabus for 2026 to 2028)

| | AS Level Paper 1 |
|---|---|
| Questions | 30 multiple choice, answer all |
| Time | 1 hour |
| Marks | 30 |
| Weighting | 33% of the AS Level |
| Content | AS Level topics |
| Answers | On a separate answer sheet |

That is two minutes a question, which sounds generous until you meet a question that needs a diagram and a calculation. If you continue to the full A Level, Paper 3 is the multiple-choice paper at that stage: 30 questions in 1 hour 15 minutes, on A Level content, assuming the AS material.

In the June 2024 examiner report for Paper 11, Cambridge says most candidates answered between 14 and 23 questions correctly: nine marks between students sitting the same paper. Technique on the harder questions is the part of that gap you can close fastest.

## What is new at AS

### Two things happen at once

O Level questions mostly move one curve at a time. At AS, demand and supply often shift together, or for a tax to be added to a market where you also need the elasticity. When two curves move, one of price or quantity is often **uncertain**: it depends on which shift is bigger. An option that says "cannot be determined" is not a trick. Sometimes it is the only correct answer.

### Every option is true, but only one answers the question

Easier questions catch you with a false statement. Harder ones give you four true statements about economics, and only one of them answers what was asked. Before choosing, reread the stem and ask: does this option explain **the change in the question**, or is it just correct?

### The calculation is the second step, not the first

A question on tax incidence might ask for the share paid by producers, not consumers. A comparative advantage question might ask for the terms of trade, not who should specialise. Do the calculation, then check that your number is the one the question wants.

## A method for two-step questions

1. **Name the concept** in the margin: "incidence", "XED", "comparative advantage".
2. **Write the first step** as a short line: "consumers pay the price rise".
3. **Write the second step**: "producers pay the rest of the tax".
4. **Only now look at the options.** The wrong ones are usually the answer to step 1 alone.

## Six practice questions

These are my own questions, written in the Paper 1 style. Cover the answer and try each one first.

**Question A.** A specific tax of $2 per unit is placed on a good. The market price rises from $10.00 to $11.50. What share of the tax is paid by producers?

A 25%  
B 50%  
C 75%  
D 100%

**Answer: A.** Consumers pay the price rise, $1.50 of the $2 tax. Producers pay the other $0.50: 0.50 ÷ 2 × 100 = 25%. Option C is the consumers' share, which is the answer to step 1 alone.

**Question B.** Incomes rise by 10% and the quantity demanded of a good falls by 5%. What type of good is it, and what is its income elasticity of demand?

A a normal good with YED of +0.5  
B an inferior good with YED of −0.5  
C an inferior good with YED of −2  
D a luxury good with YED of +2

**Answer: B.** YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income = −5 ÷ 10 = −0.5. A negative YED means an inferior good. Option C divides the wrong way round.

**Question C.** The price of good X rises by 20% and the quantity demanded of good Y rises by 10%. What is the cross elasticity of demand, and how are the goods related?

A +0.5, substitutes  
B +0.5, complements  
C −0.5, complements  
D +2, substitutes

**Answer: A.** XED = % change in quantity demanded of Y ÷ % change in price of X = 10 ÷ 20 = +0.5. A positive XED means substitutes: X became dearer, so people bought more Y.

**Question D.** With the same resources, Country P can produce either 10 cars or 20 tonnes of wheat. Country Q can produce either 6 cars or 18 tonnes of wheat. Which terms of trade would allow both countries to gain if P exports cars and Q exports wheat?

A 1 car for 1.5 tonnes of wheat  
B 1 car for 2.5 tonnes of wheat  
C 1 car for 3.5 tonnes of wheat  
D 1 car for 4 tonnes of wheat

**Answer: B.** The opportunity cost of 1 car is 2 tonnes of wheat in P (20 ÷ 10) and 3 tonnes in Q (18 ÷ 6). P has the comparative advantage in cars. Both gain only if 1 car trades for between 2 and 3 tonnes of wheat, and only B is in that range.

**Question E.** In a floating exchange rate system, what is most likely to cause a country's currency to appreciate?

A a rise in its imports  
B a fall in foreign demand for its exports  
C a rise in its interest rates compared with other countries  
D a rise in its inflation rate compared with other countries

**Answer: C.** Higher interest rates attract saving from abroad, which raises demand for the currency. A, B and D all reduce demand for the currency or raise its supply, so each would push it down.

**Question F.** At the same time, demand for a good increases and its supply increases. What must happen in the market?

A price rises and quantity rises  
B price falls and quantity rises  
C quantity rises, but price may rise, fall or stay the same  
D price and quantity may each rise, fall or stay the same

**Answer: C.** Both shifts push quantity up, so quantity must rise. They push price in opposite directions, so the change in price depends on which shift is bigger. A and B are each possible, but neither **must** happen. D is wrong because quantity is certain.

<Tip>
When a question shifts two curves, draw both shifts on the paper and write an arrow for price and for quantity under each one. If the two arrows for price point in opposite directions, the price change is uncertain, and you are looking for an option that says so.
</Tip>

## Practising by topic, not by paper

Full past papers matter, but at AS it pays to log your wrong answers by **topic** as well as by type of mistake. Paper 1 draws on the whole AS syllabus, so after three or four papers you will see which areas keep costing you: elasticities, government intervention, the macroeconomy or international trade. Then do ten questions on just that area before the next full paper.

If the same topic keeps going wrong, book a trial lesson and bring your marked papers. We will go through them together.
