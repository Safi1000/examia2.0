A maximum price is a legal ceiling set below equilibrium, so it creates a shortage. A minimum price is a legal floor set above equilibrium, so it creates a surplus. A specific indirect tax shifts supply up by the tax per unit and raises the price; a subsidy shifts supply down and lowers it. Who bears the tax, or gains from the subsidy, depends on price elasticity of demand and supply.

## What does the 9708 syllabus ask you to know?

These tools sit in section 3 of the 9708 syllabus for 2026 to 2028, government microeconomic intervention. Section 3.1.3 gives controlling prices as one reason to intervene. Section 3.2 lists the methods.

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 3.2.1 Specific indirect taxes | Show the impact and the incidence: who pays the tax in the end |
| 3.2.2 Subsidies | Show the impact and the incidence: who gains from it |
| 3.2.4 Maximum and minimum prices | Show the shortage or surplus and judge the policy |
| 3.2.3, 3.2.5, 3.2.6 | Direct provision, buffer stocks and provision of information, the other methods |

Note the word **specific**. The syllabus names specific indirect taxes, a fixed amount per unit, such as $3 a litre. Learn that diagram first.

## Worked example: one market, four interventions

I use one market for every diagram, so you can see how each tool moves the same curves. The figures are my own.

> The market for cooking oil, price in $ per litre, quantity in thousand litres a week:

| Price ($) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 140 | 20 |
| 8 | 120 | 60 |
| 10 | 100 | 100 |
| 12 | 80 | 140 |
| 14 | 60 | 180 |

Equilibrium is $10 and 100 thousand litres. Demand falls by 10 for each $1 rise in price. Supply rises by 20 for each $1 rise.

## How does a maximum price work?

A maximum price is a legal limit: sellers cannot charge more than it. To have any effect, it must be set **below** equilibrium. Set above equilibrium, it changes nothing, because the market price is already lower.

Say the government sets a maximum price of $8 a litre.

- Quantity demanded extends from 100 to 120.
- Quantity supplied contracts from 100 to 60.
- There is a **shortage of 60** thousand litres.
- Only 60 thousand litres are bought, fewer than the 100 at equilibrium.

**The diagram in words.** Draw demand sloping down and supply sloping up, crossing at Pe ($10) and Qe (100). Draw a horizontal line below Pe, labelled Pmax ($8). Where it meets supply, drop a line to Qs (60). Where it meets demand, drop a line to Qd (120). Label the gap between Qs and Qd "shortage". You can see this drawn in my [economics diagrams tool](/tools/economics-diagrams).

### Who gains and who loses?

Consumers who still get the oil pay $2 less. That is the aim: to make an essential good affordable. But 60 thousand litres of demand is not met at all. Some households get nothing.

**Explain the shortage, do not just show it.** The June 2023 Paper 22 report said many answers showed the shortage on a diagram with only "quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied" as the reason. Better answers explained that the lower price gives producers less incentive to supply, because their profit margins fall. The report said many answers stayed at Level 2 that could have reached Level 3 with that explanation.

Because the price cannot ration the oil, something else will:

- queues, or first come first served
- sellers choosing favoured customers
- rationing by the government, such as coupons
- illegal markets, where oil is sold above $8

The November 2022 Paper 22 report said the least successful answers asserted shortages and illegal markets without explaining them. Say why each one happens: the price can no longer clear the market, so buyers who are willing to pay more find another way.

In the March 2026 Paper 22 scheme for Question 2(a), the analysis marks needed both sides: the lower price helps those who buy, but the shortage means only those who can get the reduced supply benefit. One side only was capped at 2 of the 3 analysis marks.

Paper 1 tests the same logic. In June 2022 Paper 13 Question 17, a maximum price was set below equilibrium for a product. The report said option A, that some consumers would be unable to buy, was correct, and that 32 per cent chose B, that firms would produce more to meet the excess demand. Firms produce less at a lower price, not more. In November 2022 Paper 12 Question 15, the report explained that you cannot conclude consumption rises: quantity demanded rises, but only the smaller quantity supplied is actually bought.

## How does a minimum price work?

A minimum price is a legal limit: sellers cannot charge less than it. To have any effect, it must be set **above** equilibrium.

Say the government sets a minimum price of $12 a litre.

- Quantity supplied extends from 100 to 140.
- Quantity demanded contracts from 100 to 80.
- There is a **surplus of 60** thousand litres.

**The diagram in words.** Draw a horizontal line above Pe, labelled Pmin ($12). Where it meets demand, drop to Qd (80). Where it meets supply, drop to Qs (140). Label the gap "surplus".

If the government promises to buy the surplus to keep the price up, it spends 60 thousand × $12 = $720,000 a week. That money has an opportunity cost: it could have been spent on schools or hospitals.

Reasons for a minimum price, which the March 2023 Paper 22 scheme credited, include protecting producers' incomes and making demerit goods dearer to cut consumption. Its problems include the surplus and producers becoming less efficient because their price is protected.

**Mark the surplus.** The March 2023 Paper 22 report said some minimum price diagrams did not show the surplus. It also said candidates mentioned the surplus without explaining why it is a problem, "as though its existence was learned but not understood". In that scheme, a missing or inaccurate diagram capped the answer at Level 2.

### A rise in an existing minimum price

Read the question word for word. In November 2022 Paper 22 Question 1(d), the data described a rise in a minimum price that already existed. The scheme gave up to 2 marks for a diagram showing the floor moving up and supply extending, then 1 mark for explaining that the higher guaranteed price encourages farmers to produce more. A diagram showing a brand new minimum price earned at most 1 mark overall. The report said many candidates drew a new minimum price anyway.

<Tip title="Above or below?">
Maximum price below equilibrium, minimum price above. The November 2022 Paper 21 report said some candidates drew a minimum price below equilibrium. The March 2026 Paper 22 report said some placed a maximum price above it. Either one makes the diagram show no effect at all.
</Tip>

## How does a specific indirect tax work?

A specific tax is a fixed amount on each unit. The **impact** of the tax is on whoever must pay it to the government, usually the producer. The **incidence** is who really bears it in the end, through the price.

Say the government puts a specific tax of $3 a litre on cooking oil, paid by producers.

**The diagram in words.** The supply curve shifts **up** (to the left) by exactly $3 at every quantity, and stays parallel to the old one. Producers now need $3 more at each output to supply the same amount. Demand does not move.

With my figures, the 60 thousand litres producers offered at $8 are now offered only at $11, and so on along the curve. The new equilibrium is:

| | Before the tax | After the tax |
|---|---|---|
| Price consumers pay | $10 | $12 |
| Price producers keep (after paying $3) | $10 | $9 |
| Quantity | 100 thousand | 80 thousand |

Check it: at $12, demand is 80. Producers keep $9, and at $9 the original supply schedule gives 20 × 9 − 100 = 80. Both sides match.

### Who pays the tax?

- Consumers pay $2 more per litre: $2 of the $3.
- Producers keep $1 less per litre: $1 of the $3.
- Tax revenue: $3 × 80 thousand = $240,000 a week. Consumers bear $160,000 of it and producers $80,000.

On the diagram, the tax revenue is the rectangle between $12 and $9, out to 80 thousand litres. Split it with a horizontal line at the old price, $10: the top part is the consumers' share, the bottom part the producers'.

### Why did consumers bear more?

At the starting point, demand changes by 10 per cent when price changes by 10 per cent, so PED is 1 there (ignoring the minus sign). Supply changes by 20 per cent, so PES is 2. Supply is more elastic than demand, so producers can cut output easily and push more of the tax onto consumers.

The general rule:

| Situation | Who bears more of the tax |
|---|---|
| Demand price inelastic, supply elastic | Consumers |
| Demand price elastic, supply inelastic | Producers |
| Demand perfectly inelastic | Consumers bear all of it |

The June 2022 Paper 21 scheme for the tax incidence essay set it out this way: the burden depends on PED and on the elasticity of supply. The report said most candidates analysed this well, but little evaluation was given on whether the burden "always" falls on the producer.

The November 2022 Paper 23 report said some sales tax diagrams had wrong labels, confused supply and demand, or shifted supply to the right as if for a subsidy. It said answers that only described the diagram usually earned 2 marks at most out of 4.

## How does a subsidy work?

A subsidy is a payment by the government to producers for each unit. It lowers their costs, so supply shifts **down** (to the right) by the subsidy per unit.

Say the government pays a subsidy of $1.50 a litre to producers.

| | Before the subsidy | After the subsidy |
|---|---|---|
| Price consumers pay | $10 | $9 |
| Price producers receive (including $1.50) | $10 | $10.50 |
| Quantity | 100 thousand | 110 thousand |

Check it: at $9, demand is 110. Producers receive $10.50, and at $10.50 the original supply schedule gives 20 × 10.5 − 100 = 110. Both sides match.

- Consumers gain $1 per litre: $1 of the $1.50.
- Producers gain $0.50 per litre: $0.50 of the $1.50.
- Cost to the government: $1.50 × 110 thousand = $165,000 a week.

The same elasticity rule applies in reverse. Here demand is less elastic than supply, so consumers get most of the benefit.

**Show both prices on the diagram.** In November 2022 Paper 22 Question 1(e), the scheme needed the fall in the price consumers pay and the rise in the price producers receive for both diagram marks. The report said many candidates treated it as a question on consumer and producer surplus, and often gained only 1 mark, for the shift and the fall in price. A justified conclusion earned the last mark.

The cost to the government grows with the quantity sold after the subsidy. In November 2022 Paper 13 Question 16, the cost was smallest when demand and supply were both price inelastic, because sales rise least. The report said this was the question answered least well on that paper.

## How are these questions marked?

Two marking systems appear on Paper 2.

**Point based parts.** Short data response parts and the 8 mark essay part list marks for each skill. In the March 2026 Paper 22 scheme for the 8 mark maximum price question, there were 3 marks for knowledge and understanding (the definition, an accurately labelled diagram with the ceiling below equilibrium, and the change in quantities), 3 for analysis, and 2 for evaluation, with the second evaluation mark kept for a justified conclusion.

**Levels for the 12 mark part.** In the March 2023 and June 2023 Paper 22 schemes for the 12 mark questions, knowledge, understanding and analysis were marked out of 8 using one levels table, and evaluation out of 4 using another. In both schemes, a one sided answer could reach Level 2 at most. In the March 2023 scheme, a missing or inaccurate diagram also capped the answer at Level 2.

My guide to [9708 8 and 12 mark answers](/blog/as-level-economics-9708-8-and-12-mark-answers) shows how the levels work across all topics.

### What counts as evaluation here?

Evaluation means a judgement, not a summary. The November 2023 Paper 21 report said a number of candidates only summarised points already made in a question on whether a maximum price is always advantageous. Good evaluation on this topic asks:

- How far below or above equilibrium is the price set? A small gap means a small shortage or surplus.
- How elastic are demand and supply? Elastic curves make the shortage or surplus bigger.
- Can the government enforce it, and what does that cost?
- Is there a better tool, such as transfer payments, a subsidy or direct provision? The November 2022 Paper 22 report said alternative policies earn no credit unless the question asks for a comparison or the best policy.
- Short run or long run? Supply often becomes more elastic over time, so a shortage can grow.

## What do examiners say students get wrong?

| Mistake | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Shortage shown on the diagram but not explained (often, June 2023 P22) | Explain why producers supply less at the lower price |
| Shortages and illegal markets asserted (the least successful answers, November 2022 P22) | Explain how each one arises |
| Minimum price diagram with no surplus marked (March 2023 P22) | Label Qd, Qs and the surplus |
| Minimum price drawn below equilibrium (some, November 2022 P21) | Floor above, ceiling below |
| A new minimum price drawn when the data showed a rise (many, November 2022 P22) | Read the data: show the old and the new floor |
| Subsidy diagram without the producer's price (only a minority correct, November 2022 P22) | Show the price consumers pay and the price producers receive |
| Tax diagram with supply shifted right (some, November 2022 P23) | A tax shifts supply up and to the left |
| Thinking firms produce more under a maximum price (32 per cent chose this, June 2022 P13) | Firms supply less at a lower price |

## Practice questions with answers

Each question is my own. Try it on paper before reading the answer.

### Practice question 1

> The market for wheat flour has equilibrium at $5 per kg and 500 tonnes. At $4 per kg, quantity demanded is 600 tonnes and quantity supplied is 350 tonnes. The government sets a maximum price of $4 per kg.
>
> (a) Calculate the shortage. [1]
>
> (b) Explain one reason why the government might do this and one problem it may cause. [4]

**Answer**

(a) 600 minus 350 = 250 tonnes.

(b) Flour is a staple food, so a lower price makes it affordable for poorer households, who spend a large share of income on food. But at $4 producers supply 150 tonnes less than at equilibrium, because the lower price cuts their profit margin. With 250 tonnes of demand unmet, flour has to be rationed by queues, or it may be resold in an illegal market above $4, so some poor households still go without.

### Practice question 2

> A specific tax of $2 per unit is placed on a good. The price consumers pay rises from $5.00 to $6.50, and the quantity falls from 400 to 340 units.
>
> (a) Calculate the share of the tax per unit paid by consumers and by producers. [2]
>
> (b) Calculate the total tax revenue and the part paid by consumers. [2]
>
> (c) What does this suggest about PED compared with PES? [1]

**Answer**

(a) Consumers pay $1.50 more. Producers keep $6.50 minus $2 = $4.50, which is $0.50 less than before. Consumers bear $1.50 and producers $0.50 of each $2.

(b) Revenue = $2 × 340 = $680. Consumers pay $1.50 × 340 = $510.

(c) Consumers bear more, so demand is less price elastic than supply.

### Practice question 3

> With the help of a diagram, explain how a subsidy to producers of solar panels affects the market, and consider whether consumers gain more than producers. [8]

**Answer outline**

- Define a subsidy as a payment per unit from the government to producers, which lowers their costs.
- Diagram: supply shifts down and to the right by the subsidy per unit. Show the new, lower price consumers pay, the higher price producers receive (new price plus subsidy), and the rise in quantity.
- Analysis: consumers gain the fall in price; producers gain the rise in the price they receive; the vertical gap is the subsidy per unit.
- Evaluation: if demand for solar panels is price elastic, producers gain more; if it is price inelastic, consumers gain more. Conclude with a judgement for this market, such as whether many buyers are price sensitive households.

The marks and outlines here are my own judgement for practice, not a Cambridge scheme.

## FAQ

### Is a maximum price above or below equilibrium?

Below. A maximum price set above the equilibrium price has no effect, because the market price is already lower than the ceiling.

### What is the difference between the impact and the incidence of a tax?

The impact is on whoever hands the tax to the government, usually the producer. The incidence is who really bears it in the end, shared between consumers and producers through the price.

### Who pays more of an indirect tax?

Whichever side is less price elastic. If demand is inelastic, consumers pay more. If supply is inelastic, producers pay more.

### Does a subsidy shift supply or demand?

Supply. A subsidy to producers lowers their costs, so the supply curve shifts down and to the right by the subsidy per unit.

### What happens to the surplus under a minimum price?

It may be bought by the government, stored, destroyed or sold abroad. Each has a cost, which is a key point in any evaluation.

### Do I need to know ad valorem taxes for AS 9708?

The 2026 to 2028 syllabus names specific indirect taxes in section 3.2.1. Learn the specific tax diagram first. Check the official syllabus if your teacher sets ad valorem questions.
