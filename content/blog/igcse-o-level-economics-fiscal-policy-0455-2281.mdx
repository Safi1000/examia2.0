Fiscal policy is the government's use of taxation and government spending to influence total demand and reach its macroeconomic aims. For IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 you need the government budget and how to calculate a deficit, why governments spend and tax, the types of tax and their effects, and how fiscal policy helps or harms each aim.

## What does the syllabus ask you to know about fiscal policy?

Fiscal policy is topic 4.2 in both the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029. The content is the same in both.

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 4.2.1 Government budget | Define the budget, a budget deficit and a budget surplus; calculate the size of a deficit or surplus |
| 4.2.2 Reasons for government spending | Explain the main areas of spending, why governments spend in them and the effects |
| 4.2.3 Taxation | Explain six reasons for taxation, classify taxes, and explain their impact on consumers, workers, firms, the government and the economy |
| 4.2.4 Definition of fiscal policy | Define it |
| 4.2.5 Fiscal policy measures | Explain changes in taxes and government spending |
| 4.2.6 Effects on macroeconomic aims | Explain how fiscal policy may help a government achieve its aims |

The aims themselves sit in topic 4.1, so you will use both topics together.

Two changes matter here. The syllabus changes page lists old topic 4.3.5, principles of taxation, under content "removed or significantly reduced". It also says reasons for taxation are now covered more explicitly. So I would not make old questions on the qualities of a good tax, such as one in the June 2024 0455 Paper 22, a priority now. You can tick off each point on the [0455 syllabus checklist](/syllabus/igcse-economics-0455).

## What is fiscal policy?

> Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence total demand in the economy and achieve the government's macroeconomic aims.

None of the 0455 or 2281 Paper 2 question papers I hold from 2022 to 2026 asks you to define fiscal policy. The November 2025 0455 Paper 21 did ask candidates to identify two fiscal policy measures, and its mark scheme credited government spending and taxation. Those two words must be in your definition.

There are two directions:

| | Expansionary fiscal policy | Contractionary fiscal policy |
|---|---|---|
| Taxes | Cut | Raise |
| Government spending | Raise | Cut |
| Effect on total demand | Increases | Decreases |
| Used to | Raise output and employment in a slowdown or recession | Reduce demand pull inflation or a large budget deficit |
| Effect on the budget | Moves towards a deficit | Moves towards a surplus |

Keep fiscal policy with the right body. The government sets taxes and its own spending. Interest rates and the money supply are monetary policy, usually run by the central bank. The March 2023 0455 examiner report (Paper 22) noted that some candidates were unsure what a central bank does, and a number of those had it using fiscal policy against inflation.

## What is the government budget, and how do you calculate a deficit?

The June 2023 0455 Paper 23 mark scheme gave the 2 marks for three ideas, which I put this way: the **government budget** is the government's plan, or forecast, of what it will spend and what it will receive.

- A **budget deficit** is when government spending is greater than government revenue.
- A **budget surplus** is when government revenue is greater than government spending.

The 2022 to 2026 Paper 2 papers I hold do not ask for these two as definitions. The June 2022 0455 Paper 22 scheme did credit "government spending was greater than government revenue", then a mark for naming this a budget deficit.

The calculation is one line:

> Budget balance = government revenue minus government spending

A positive answer is a surplus. A negative answer is a deficit. The same formula is on my [0455 formula sheet](/tools/formula-sheet/igcse-economics-0455).

### Worked example: a budget deficit as a percentage of GDP

Country Y's figures for one year are below. They are my own.

| Revenue | $bn | Spending | $bn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 14 | Education | 9 |
| Corporation tax | 6 | Healthcare | 8 |
| Sales tax | 13 | State benefits and pensions | 11 |
| Import tariffs | 3 | Infrastructure | 7 |
| | | Defence and other | 7 |
| **Total revenue** | **36** | **Total spending** | **42** |

**Step 1.** Add each side: revenue 14 + 6 + 13 + 3 = $36bn; spending 9 + 8 + 11 + 7 + 7 = $42bn.

**Step 2.** Budget balance = 36 minus 42 = minus $6bn. Spending is higher, so this is a **budget deficit of $6bn**.

**Step 3.** Country Y's GDP is $150bn. Deficit as a percentage of GDP = 6 ÷ 150 × 100 = **4%**.

The November 2023 0455 Paper 23 mark scheme used this method: the deficit divided by GDP.

Now suppose the government raises sales tax so that revenue from it rises by $2bn, and cuts infrastructure spending by $1bn. Revenue becomes $38bn and spending $41bn. The deficit falls to $3bn, which is 2% of GDP. In a real economy the higher sales tax may cut spending, so revenue may rise by less than $2bn. That is the kind of point that earns marks in a discuss answer.

## Why do governments spend money?

Government spending pays for things the market would not provide, or would provide too little of, and it is a tool for the aims. The syllabus does not list the areas. These are the ones I teach.

| Area | Why governments spend on it | Possible effects |
|---|---|---|
| Education and training | A merit good; raises skills | Higher productivity and incomes; more growth in the long run |
| Healthcare | A merit good; healthier workers | Higher life expectancy; fewer days lost to illness |
| Infrastructure (roads, ports, power) | Firms need it but would underprovide it | Lower costs for firms; better mobility of labour |
| State benefits and pensions | Support people with low or no income | Less poverty; more equal distribution of income |
| Subsidies to firms | Lower costs of chosen industries | Lower prices; more output and jobs in those industries |
| Defence and law and order | Public goods the market will not supply | Security that allows the economy to work |

Every area has an opportunity cost. Money spent on one area cannot be spent on another, and more spending must be paid for by higher taxes, borrowing or a larger deficit.

The November 2023 2281 examiner report (Paper 23) said the stronger answers did not treat spending as one lump. They matched an area, such as training or subsidies to firms, to the type of unemployment it works on. Name the area of spending and follow its own chain.

## Why do governments tax?

A **tax** is a compulsory payment to the government, by people or firms, on income, spending or profits, which provides government revenue. That follows the June 2026 0455 Paper 21 mark scheme, which listed four points for the 2 marks: a payment, made to the government, who pays or what is taxed, and revenue. The scheme puts the idea of compulsory in brackets, so it helps but is not required.

The syllabus lists six reasons for taxation. Learn all six.

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| Raising revenue | Income tax pays for schools and hospitals |
| Discouraging consumption of demerit goods | A high tax on cigarettes raises their price |
| Reducing imports | An import tariff makes foreign goods dearer than home produced goods |
| Redistributing income | A progressive income tax takes a larger share from the rich, and the revenue funds benefits |
| Influencing total demand | Higher taxes reduce spending when the economy is growing too fast |
| Encouraging environmental sustainability | A tax on pollution gives firms a reason to cut emissions |

The 2027 specimen Paper 2 asked candidates to discuss a carbon tax. Its mark scheme credited both sides: a carbon tax may cut emissions and external costs, but it may be regressive and may make a country's products less competitive if other countries do not tax carbon.

## What are the different types of tax?

The syllabus asks for two ways of classifying taxes. The November 2023 0455 Paper 21 mark scheme accepted any of these five types when a question asked to identify two types of tax.

### Direct and indirect taxes

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Direct tax | A tax on income, wealth or profits, paid straight to the government by the person or firm it is placed on | Personal income tax, corporation tax, a tax on wealth |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending on goods and services, collected through sellers and usually passed on to buyers in higher prices | Sales tax, VAT, GST, import tariffs |

The indirect tax definition follows the November 2022 0455 Paper 23 mark scheme: one mark for a tax on goods and services, one for an example such as VAT, GST or a tariff. The scheme also told examiners to give nothing for calling it a tax on a third party. The direct tax examples come from the June 2025 0455 Paper 22 scheme, which credited income tax, a tax on wealth and corporation tax.

### Progressive, regressive and proportional taxes

These are about the **share** of income paid in tax, not the amount.

| Type | What happens to the share of income paid in tax as income rises |
|---|---|
| Progressive | The share rises: the rich pay a larger percentage of their income |
| Proportional | The share stays the same for every income group |
| Regressive | The share falls: the poor pay a larger percentage of their income |

The November 2022 0455 Paper 22 mark scheme (Question 3(b)) rewarded two ideas for a progressive income tax: richer people lose a bigger percentage of their income, and the rate climbs as income climbs. For a proportional tax it rewarded an equal percentage whatever the income.

### Worked example: classifying taxes from a table

Three households in Country Y pay three different taxes. The figures are my own.

**Tax 1: income tax**

| Household | Income per year | Income tax paid | Share of income |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $7,200 | $360 | 5% |
| B | $18,000 | $1,800 | 10% |
| C | $45,000 | $9,000 | 20% |

Work out each share: 360 ÷ 7,200 × 100 = 5%; 1,800 ÷ 18,000 × 100 = 10%; 9,000 ÷ 45,000 × 100 = 20%. The share rises with income, so this tax is **progressive**.

**Tax 2: a flat 12% tax on income**

Household A pays $864, B pays $2,160 and C pays $5,400. Each is 12% of income, so the tax is **proportional**. Notice that C still pays far more dollars than A. That is why the amount alone never tells you the type.

**Tax 3: a 10% sales tax on basic food**

Each household spends $1,440 a year on basic food, so each pays $144 in tax.

| Household | Income per year | Sales tax paid | Share of income |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $7,200 | $144 | 2% |
| B | $18,000 | $144 | 0.8% |
| C | $45,000 | $144 | 0.32% |

The same dollar amount is a bigger share of a small income. The share falls as income rises, so this tax is **regressive**.

Be careful with that last point. Indirect taxes on necessities are often regressive, but "indirect" and "regressive" do not mean the same thing. The November 2022 0455 Paper 23 examiner report pointed out that being regressive "could equally be true of some direct taxes".

<Tip title="Amount or percentage?">
Every income tax system, even a regressive one, can take more dollars from the rich. The November 2023 2281 examiner report (Paper 22) made exactly this point. Always write about the percentage of income, and show it with figures if the question gives any.
</Tip>

## How do taxes affect consumers, workers, firms, the government and the economy?

The syllabus names five groups. If a question names one, write about that group only.

| Group | Possible effects of higher taxes |
|---|---|
| Consumers | Indirect taxes raise prices; direct taxes cut disposable income, so spending falls |
| Workers | Higher income tax cuts take home pay and may reduce the incentive to work |
| Firms | Higher corporation tax cuts the profit firms keep, so investment and expansion may fall; indirect taxes may reduce sales |
| The government | More revenue to spend, but very high rates may encourage tax evasion and bring in less |
| The economy | Lower total demand can reduce inflation but also output and employment; progressive taxes make incomes more equal |

The June 2025 0455 Paper 22 mark scheme for a question on direct taxes and unemployment shows how a chain is built. Higher income tax cuts disposable income, then consumer spending and total demand. Because the demand for workers is derived from the demand for goods, firms need fewer workers, so cyclical unemployment rises. Its report added a warning about some answers: if higher taxes make workers emigrate, employment may fall, but unemployment does not rise.

### Does a higher tax always reduce inflation?

No. A rise in income tax cuts disposable income and can reduce demand pull inflation. A rise in indirect taxes adds to prices directly. The June 2022 reports for both 0455 and 2281 (Paper 21) noted a few candidates who mixed up income tax with a tax on products and said higher income tax would raise prices.

A June 2023 multiple choice question (0455 and 2281 Paper 12, Question 17) tested this. It asked what a government intended by raising indirect taxes while leaving direct taxes unchanged. The correct answer was to reduce a budget deficit. In 0455, 33% chose it and 36% chose reducing inflation instead.

## How does fiscal policy affect the government's macroeconomic aims?

The syllabus lists six aims in 4.1. Here is how fiscal policy can help each one.

| Aim | How fiscal policy can help |
|---|---|
| Economic growth | Lower taxes or higher spending raise total demand and output; spending on education and infrastructure raises productive capacity |
| Full employment | Higher total demand raises the derived demand for labour, so cyclical unemployment falls |
| Stable prices | Higher income tax or lower spending reduce total demand and demand pull inflation |
| Balance of payments stability | Lower demand means less spending on imports; tariffs make imports dearer |
| Redistribution of income | Progressive taxes and state benefits narrow the gap between rich and poor |
| Environmental sustainability | Taxes on pollution, and spending on cleaner energy, change what firms and consumers do |

The March 2022 0455 examiner report (Paper 22) noted some candidates who answered an unemployment question with a tax on demerit goods, a policy not aimed at unemployment. Match each measure to the aim in the question.

### Why might fiscal policy not work?

This is what earns the second side of a discuss answer. Points 1 to 3 and 6 draw on the 0455 March 2026 Paper 22, June 2023 Paper 23 and November 2025 Paper 22 mark schemes, point 5 on the 0455 June 2025 Paper 22 scheme, and points 4 and 7 are my own teaching.

1. **Conflicts between aims.** Expansionary policy can cause demand pull inflation, especially close to full employment. The syllabus lists full employment and stable prices as a possible conflict.
2. **The budget.** Tax cuts reduce government revenue, so the government may later have less to spend on education, healthcare or infrastructure.
3. **Tax evasion.** Higher rates may lead people to hide income, so revenue falls.
4. **Confidence.** If households and firms are worried, they may save a tax cut rather than spend it.
5. **Incentives.** Higher direct taxes may reduce the incentive to work and invest.
6. **Imports.** Some of the extra spending goes on imports, which can worsen the current account.
7. **Time.** New spending takes time to plan, and education takes years to raise productivity.

## How are fiscal policy questions marked in Paper 2?

From 2027, Paper 2 for both 0455 and 2281 is 2 hours and 80 marks. Section A is one compulsory question with parts (a) to (f). In Section B you answer three questions out of four. In the 2027 specimen paper, each Section B question has parts worth 2, 4, 6 and 8 marks, and Section A ended with a 6 mark discuss question on a carbon tax.

| Part (specimen) | Command word | How the specimen mark scheme gives marks |
|---|---|---|
| (a) 2 marks | Define, identify | Precise points |
| (b) 4 marks | Explain | In the three "explain two" parts, 1 for identifying each point and 1 for explaining it |
| (c) 6 marks | Analyse | In the policy question, 2 for naming two policies, the rest for steps in the chain |
| (d) 8 marks | Discuss | Level 1 (1 to 2), Level 2 (3 to 5, may be one sided), Level 3 (6 to 8, both sides developed) |

One rule in the specimen mark scheme is easy to miss. It sits in the guidance for the 6 mark discuss question in Section A: a point earns credit once only, whichever side you put it on. The scheme's own example is about tax revenue. Saying revenue may rise because incomes are higher, after saying it may fall because incomes are lower, scores nothing. A different reason earns the mark. The March 2026 0455 Paper 22 scheme applies the same rule to every discuss question on that paper.

My guide on [how to answer IGCSE and O Level Economics questions](/blog/how-to-answer-igcse-o-level-economics-questions) covers each command word in full.

### Model answer: an "explain two" question

**Question (mine):** Explain two reasons why a government may raise the tax on imported cars. [4]

> **To reduce imports.** The tax raises the price of imported cars compared with cars made at home, so consumers buy fewer imported cars and more domestic ones, which protects domestic car makers and jobs.
>
> **To encourage environmental sustainability.** A higher price reduces the number of cars bought, so there is less fuel burnt and less air pollution.

This follows the specimen's pattern for "explain two" parts: each paragraph names a reason from the syllabus list (1 mark) and explains how the tax achieves it (1 mark).

### Model answer: a "discuss" question

**Question (mine):** Discuss whether or not a government should raise income tax rates to reduce a budget deficit. [8]

> **Why it should.** A budget deficit means government spending is greater than revenue. If incomes stay the same, higher income tax rates raise more revenue from each worker, so the gap between revenue and spending narrows. Unlike cutting spending, this protects schools, hospitals and benefits. If the rise falls mainly on higher incomes, the tax becomes more progressive, which also helps redistribute income. With a smaller deficit the government needs to borrow less, so less revenue goes on interest in future.
>
> **Why it should not.** Higher income tax cuts disposable income, so consumer spending and total demand fall. Firms sell less, so profits fall and some workers lose their jobs. Revenue from sales tax and corporation tax then falls, and the government spends more on unemployment benefits, so the deficit may shrink by far less than expected. Higher rates may also reduce the incentive to work, and some people may evade the tax.
>
> **Judgement.** Raising income tax is most likely to reduce the deficit when the economy is growing strongly and close to full employment, because the fall in demand then also reduces inflationary pressure. In a recession it may deepen the slowdown and cut other tax revenue, so the deficit may hardly fall. A small rise aimed at high earners is less risky than a large rise for everyone.

This is written to meet the Level 3 description: both sides are developed with chains of reasoning, and the judgement says what the outcome depends on. Notice the second side uses new reasons, not the first side reversed.

## Where do students lose marks on fiscal policy?

These come from 0455 and 2281 examiner reports from 2022 to 2026. The wording in the middle column is my summary.

| Mistake | What to do instead | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Confusing a government budget deficit with a current account deficit | Budget: government revenue and spending. Current account: exports and imports | 0455 November 2022 Paper 22 (Questions 1(e), 1(g), 5(b)); 0455 June 2023 Paper 21 (a small number); 0455 March 2025 Paper 22, Question 5(d) (some); 0455 June 2025 Paper 22, Question 1(a) (some); 2281 November 2023 Paper 22, Question 5(d) (only a small proportion) |
| Saying a progressive tax just takes more money from the rich | Write about a larger percentage of income, and the rate rising with income | 0455 and 2281 November 2022 Paper 22, Question 3(b); 2281 November 2023 Paper 22 |
| Mixing up progressive and regressive | Progressive: share rises with income. Regressive: share falls | 0455 and 2281 November 2022 Paper 22 (a small number) |
| Defining an indirect tax only as one that is passed on to someone else | A tax on goods and services, with an example such as VAT | 0455 and 2281 November 2022 Paper 23, Question 3(a) (quite a few) |
| Treating income tax as a tax on goods | Income tax cuts disposable income; taxes on goods raise prices | 0455 and 2281 June 2022 Paper 21, Question 2(c) (a few) |
| Repeating that tax revenue is higher, with no tax named | Link each cause to its tax: income tax, sales tax, corporation tax, tariffs | 0455 and 2281 June 2023 Paper 21, Question 2(c) |
| Treating all taxes as one ("low taxes") | Separate direct and indirect taxes; their effects differ | 0455 June 2023 Paper 23, Question 2(d) (comment on a Level 2 answer) |
| Reversing the first side as the second side | Give a different reason for the other side | 0455 March 2026 Paper 22, Question 1(g) (some) |

When a question says "budget deficit", underline it and write "government spending is greater than tax revenue" in your first line. It keeps you away from exports and imports.

## Can you answer these practice questions?

Try each one before you read the answer. All three questions are my own, and the mark splits are my judgement, not Cambridge's.

**1.** Country Z's government spent $16.2bn in a year. It had a budget surplus equal to 2% of its GDP of $55bn. Calculate its government revenue. Show your working. [2]

**Answer.** Surplus = 2 ÷ 100 × 55 = $1.1bn (1). A surplus means revenue is higher than spending, so revenue = 16.2 + 1.1 = **$17.3bn** (1).

**2.** A government cuts income tax rates and raises the sales tax on food. Explain two ways this may affect a low income household. [4]

**Answer.** Food becomes dearer (1). Food takes a large share of a low income, so the extra tax is a bigger percentage of this household's income than of a rich household's, which makes the change regressive (1). The household may keep more of its pay (1), but only if it earns enough to pay income tax at all, so the gain may be small or nothing (1).

**3.** Analyse how a cut in the rate of sales tax may affect a country's firms. [6]

**Answer.** A sales tax is an indirect tax, so a cut lowers the prices consumers pay. Consumers can now buy more with the same income, so the quantity of goods demanded rises. Firms sell more, so their revenue rises, and if costs rise by less, their profits rise. Higher profits give firms both the funds and the reason to invest and expand, so they may employ more workers. Firms that buy taxed inputs also face lower costs, which can make their products more price competitive, including abroad. Firms selling products with price elastic demand gain most, because their sales rise by a larger percentage than the fall in price.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between fiscal policy and monetary policy?

Fiscal policy uses taxation and government spending and is set by the government. Monetary policy uses the interest rate, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate, and is usually run by the central bank.

### Is a budget deficit always bad?

No. A deficit can support output and jobs in a recession. It becomes a problem if it is large for many years, because the borrowing must be repaid with interest.

### What are the six reasons for taxation in the 2027 syllabus?

Raising revenue, discouraging consumption of demerit goods, reducing imports, redistributing income, influencing total demand and encouraging environmental sustainability.

### Do I still need the principles of taxation?

The 2027 to 2029 syllabus lists old topic 4.3.5, principles of taxation, under content removed or significantly reduced. Spend your time on the six reasons for taxation and the types of tax instead.

### Is VAT a regressive tax?

It can be. If poorer and richer households spend similar amounts on taxed necessities, the tax takes a larger share of the poorer household's income. Show this with a percentage, not just the dollar amount.

### Is fiscal policy the same in 0455 and 2281?

Yes. Topic 4.2 has the same content in the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029.
