Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how much the quantity supplied of a product responds to a change in its price. The formula is PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price. If PES is greater than 1, supply is elastic. If it is less than 1, supply is inelastic. Spare capacity, stocks and time decide which it is.

## What does the syllabus ask you to know about PES?

PES is topic 2.7 in both the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029. The content is the same in both:

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 2.7.1 Definition of PES | Define it |
| 2.7.2 Calculation of PES | Use the formula; interpret the value as perfectly inelastic, inelastic, unitary, elastic or perfectly elastic; draw and interpret supply curves showing different PES |
| 2.7.3 Determinants of PES | Explain the main influences on whether supply is elastic or inelastic |

The 2027 changes page lists the terms perfectly inelastic, unitary elastic and perfectly elastic under content added. So expect them in questions. You can tick off each point on the [0455 syllabus checklist](/syllabus/igcse-economics-0455).

PES is tested on both papers. The Paper 1 multiple choice questions I cite below tested the determinants and how PES changes the effect of a rise in demand, and the 2027 specimen Paper 1 has a PES question too. On Paper 2, recent questions have asked for the formula, a calculation, an "explain two influences" part and a 6 mark analysis of the determinants.

## What is the PES formula?

> PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price

Quantity goes on top, price underneath. Write quantity supplied, not just supply: the March 2026 0455 report (Paper 22) said some candidates left the word quantity out. To find each percentage change:

> Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100

Price and quantity supplied normally move in the same direction, because a higher price makes it more profitable to produce. So PES is normally a positive number. If price falls 40% and quantity supplied falls 10%, PES = −10 ÷ −40 = 0.25, still positive.

### Worked example 1: inelastic supply

A mango farmer sells mangoes by the box. These figures are my own. The price rises from $20 to $25 a box, and the quantity she supplies rises from 1 200 to 1 350 boxes a month.

**Step 1.** Percentage change in quantity supplied = (1 350 − 1 200) ÷ 1 200 × 100 = 150 ÷ 1 200 × 100 = **12.5%**.

**Step 2.** Percentage change in price = (25 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = **25%**.

**Step 3.** PES = 12.5 ÷ 25 = **0.5**.

PES is less than 1, so supply is **price inelastic**. Quantity supplied rose by a smaller percentage than price. That fits mangoes: new trees take years to bear fruit, and ripe fruit cannot be stored for long.

### Worked example 2: elastic supply

A factory makes bicycle helmets. The price rises from $16 to $18, and output rises from 640 to 800 helmets a week. These figures are my own.

- Quantity: (800 − 640) ÷ 640 × 100 = **25%**
- Price: (18 − 16) ÷ 16 × 100 = **12.5%**
- PES = 25 ÷ 12.5 = **2**

PES is greater than 1, so supply is **price elastic**. The factory can raise output quickly, perhaps because it has unused machines and a store of finished helmets.

### Working backwards from PES

Questions can give you PES and ask for the change in quantity. Rearrange the formula:

> Percentage change in quantity supplied = PES × percentage change in price

If PES is 1.5 and price rises by 8%, quantity supplied rises by 1.5 × 8 = 12%. If 2 500 units were supplied before, 12% more is 2 500 × 1.12 = 2 800 units.

You can check any of these with my [elasticity calculator](/tools/elasticity-calculator), and the formula is on the [0455 formula sheet](/tools/formula-sheet/igcse-economics-0455).

## What do the PES values mean?

| PES value | Name | What happens when price changes |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity supplied does not change at all |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity supplied changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| 1 | Unitary elastic | Quantity supplied changes by the same percentage as price |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity supplied changes by a larger percentage than price |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Producers will supply any quantity at one price, and none below it |

A PES of 0 is not just a theory. The 2027 specimen Paper 1 has a firm that cannot get the raw materials to produce more than a set amount, so its supply cannot respond to a higher price.

### What do the supply curves look like?

The syllabus asks you to draw and interpret supply curves for each type. Here is each one in words. Label the axes price and quantity, with the origin marked O.

| Type | Shape of the supply curve |
|---|---|
| Perfectly inelastic | A vertical straight line |
| Perfectly elastic | A horizontal straight line |
| Unitary elastic | A straight line starting from the origin, at any angle |
| Elastic | A straight line that meets the price axis above the origin |
| Inelastic | A straight line that meets the quantity axis to the right of the origin |

The last three rows are the rule I teach for straight line supply curves. The angle does not decide PES. Where the line meets an axis does. A steep line through the origin is still unitary elastic. The 2027 specimen mark scheme for its Paper 2 demand and supply diagram gave marks for labelled axes and labelled curves, so label every curve you draw.

## What determines the price elasticity of supply?

The first five influences are those credited in the 0455 November 2023 Paper 21 and June 2023 Paper 21 mark schemes, written in my own words with my own examples. The last comes from the 2027 specimen Paper 1 question.

| Influence | Supply is more elastic when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spare capacity | Firms have unused machines, space or workers | A factory working one shift can add a second |
| Stocks of finished goods | Firms hold unsold stock they can release | A paint maker with full warehouses |
| Ease of storage | The product can be stored cheaply and for a long time | Tinned food, compared with fresh milk |
| Time period | There is more time to respond: in the long run all factors can be changed | A fishing firm can build more boats in the long run, not next week |
| Length and difficulty of production | The product is quick and simple to make | A printed T shirt is ready in minutes; a ship takes years |
| Availability of inputs | Extra raw materials and workers are easy to get | The specimen firm that cannot get more raw materials has a PES of 0 |

**Time** links this topic to the short run and the long run. In the short run at least one factor of production is fixed, so supply is less elastic. In the long run all factors can change, so supply becomes more elastic. The November 2023 Paper 21 scheme credited exactly this chain.

### Do not mix PES and PED determinants

The influences on PED are about buyers: substitutes, necessity, the share of income spent and habit. The influences on PES are about producers: capacity, stocks, storage, time and inputs. Examiners report this mix up in both directions. My [price elasticity of demand guide](/blog/igcse-economics-price-elasticity-of-demand) covers the demand side.

## Why does PES matter?

PES decides how a change in demand affects the market. Suppose demand rises, shifting the demand curve to the right.

- If supply is **inelastic**, producers cannot raise output much, so the **price rises a lot** and quantity rises a little.
- If supply is **elastic**, producers raise output easily, so **quantity rises a lot** and price rises a little.

A November 2023 2281 multiple choice question (Paper 12, Question 6) tested exactly this. It gave four products with different PES values and asked which would see the largest price rise after the same increase in demand. The correct answer was the product with a PES of 0. Only 15% chose it, and 46% chose the product with the highest PES. The report suggested they may have confused elastic and inelastic.

This also helps you in other topics. A subsidy or a tax changes supply, and a fall in demand during a recession hits price harder where supply is inelastic. My [demand and supply guide](/blog/igcse-o-level-economics-demand-and-supply) shows how shifts and elasticity work together.

### Model answer: an "explain two" question

**Question (mine):** Explain two reasons why the supply of mangoes may be price inelastic. [4]

> **Time to grow.** Mango trees take years to produce fruit, so farmers cannot increase output quickly when the price rises. This makes supply price inelastic.
>
> **Storage.** Ripe mangoes go bad within days, so farmers cannot keep stocks to release when the price rises. Without stocks, supply cannot respond quickly, so it is price inelastic.

Each paragraph names the influence and then says what it does to PES. The second sentence is where many candidates lose the mark.

### Model answer: an "analyse" question

**Question (mine):** Analyse why a bicycle helmet factory may have price elastic supply. [6]

> The factory may have **spare capacity**, such as machines that are not used every day or workers who could do extra shifts. If the price of helmets rises, it can use these unused resources to raise output quickly, without building a new factory. So quantity supplied rises by a larger percentage than price, which means supply is elastic.
>
> Helmets can also be **stored** for a long time without going bad. The factory can build up **stocks** of finished helmets when demand is low. When the price rises, it can release these stocks onto the market at once, so quantity supplied rises straight away.
>
> Helmets are also **quick to make** from easily available materials such as plastic and foam. A short production time means extra output reaches the market soon after the price rises, so supply is more elastic.

Each paragraph follows a chain: the determinant, what it means for this factory, and the effect on how far quantity supplied can respond.

## Where do students lose marks on PES?

These come from 0455 and 2281 examiner reports. The middle column is my summary.

| Mistake | What to do instead | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Giving PED influences when asked about PES | Write about producers: capacity, stocks, storage, time | 0455 and 2281 June 2023 Paper 21, 1(d) (the main reason for a limited number of correct answers) |
| Naming an influence but not saying it makes supply elastic or inelastic | Finish every point with "so supply is more elastic" or "more inelastic" | 0455 and 2281 June 2023 Paper 21, 1(d) (many) |
| Giving PES as a percentage | PES is a number with no % sign | 0455 November 2022 Paper 23, 1(a) (quite a few) |
| Turning the formula upside down | Quantity supplied on top, price underneath | 0455 November 2022 Paper 23, 1(a) (a common error) |
| Giving PES influences, such as time, when asked about PED | Keep the two sets of influences apart | 0455 and 2281 November 2022 Paper 23, 4(a) |
| Choosing a PED factor when asked what influences PES | Spare capacity is a PES factor; necessity and share of income are PED | 2281 November 2022 Paper 13, Question 7 (34% correct) |
| Confusing elastic and inelastic supply when demand changes | Inelastic supply means a bigger price change | 2281 November 2023 Paper 12, Question 6 (15% correct) |
| Missing that longer storage raises PES | Storable products can be held as stocks, so supply is more elastic | 2281 November 2023 Paper 13, Question 7 (34% correct) |

The March 2025 0455 report (Paper 22) summed this up: candidates often understood PED well, but there seemed to be less understanding of PES.

## Can you answer these practice questions?

All three are my own. Try each one before reading the answer.

**1.** The price of a product falls by 20% and the quantity supplied falls by 6%. Calculate PES and state whether supply is elastic or inelastic. [2]

**Answer.** PES = −6 ÷ −20 = **0.3** (1). This is less than 1, so supply is **inelastic** (1).

**2.** A firm supplies 4 000 units a week. PES for its product is 1.5. The price rises by 10%. Calculate the new quantity supplied. [2]

**Answer.** Percentage change in quantity = 1.5 × 10 = 15% (1). New quantity = 4 000 × 1.15 = **4 600 units** a week (1).

**3.** Explain whether wheat or plastic bottles are likely to have the more elastic supply in the short run. [4]

**Answer.** Plastic bottles are likely to have the more elastic supply (1). A bottle factory can raise output in hours by running machines for longer, and bottles can be stored for a long time as stocks (1). Wheat takes months to grow (1), so in the short run farmers cannot raise output much after a price rise, which makes its supply inelastic (1).

## FAQ

### What is the formula for price elasticity of supply?

PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price. Work out each percentage change from the old value.

### Can PES be negative?

Not normally. Price and quantity supplied usually move in the same direction, so both percentage changes have the same sign and PES is positive.

### What does a PES of 0 mean?

Supply is perfectly inelastic. Quantity supplied does not change when price changes, and the supply curve is vertical.

### Why is supply more elastic in the long run?

In the long run all factors of production can be changed. Firms can build new factories, buy machines and train workers, so they can respond more fully to a price change.

### What is the difference between PED and PES?

PED measures how buyers respond to a price change, and it is normally negative. PES measures how producers respond, and it is normally positive. Their determinants are different too.

### Is PES the same in 0455 and 2281?

Yes. Topic 2.7 has the same content in the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029.
