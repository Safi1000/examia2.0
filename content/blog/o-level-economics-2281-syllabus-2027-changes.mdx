If you sit O Level Economics in 2027 or later, you will sit a different exam from the one your seniors sat. The changes are not dramatic, but a few of them move where your marks come from, and that should change how you revise.

Everything below comes from Cambridge's own 2027 to 2029 syllabus for 2281, compared line by line with the 2026 syllabus.

## Who the new syllabus affects

Cambridge says the 2281 syllabus "has been reviewed and revised for first examination in 2027". The new syllabus covers exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029, in the June and November series.

- **Sitting in June or November 2027 or later:** you are on the new syllabus. This includes resits. Cambridge's rule is simple: use the syllabus for the year you take the exam, not the year you started the course.
- **Sitting in November 2026:** you are still on the 2026 syllabus. Nothing in this article applies to you.
- **Using notes written for the old syllabus:** most of them still work, but check them against the lists of removed and added content below.

One more change matters as much as the content. On the 2281 syllabus page, Cambridge says the syllabus is available in administrative zones 3, 4 and 5 in 2026 and 2027, and **from 2028 in zone 4 only**. Schools in zones 3 and 5 were told in July 2026, and Cambridge suggests they may wish to consider IGCSE Economics 0455 instead. If you plan to sit in 2028 or later, ask your school's exams officer which zone it is in before you assume O Level is still an option.

## The papers: old and new side by side

Cambridge says "the assessment methods have not changed". You still sit a multiple choice paper and a structured paper, weighted 30% and 70%. What changes is the number of marks and the time.

| | 2026 syllabus | 2027 to 2029 syllabus |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper 1** Multiple Choice | 30 questions, 30 marks, 45 minutes | 40 questions, 40 marks, 1 hour |
| Paper 1 weighting | 30% | 30% |
| **Paper 2** Structured Questions | 90 marks, 2 hours 15 minutes | 80 marks, 2 hours |
| Section A | One compulsory question, 30 marks | Question 1 only, 20 marks, parts (a) to (f) |
| Section B | Three from a choice of four, 20 marks each | Three from Questions 2 to 5, 20 marks each, parts (a) to (d) |
| Paper 2 weighting | 70% | 70% |

Calculators are allowed on both papers in both versions. Grades are still A\* to E.

### What the numbers mean in practice

The pace is the same. The old Paper 1 gave you 90 seconds per question, and so does the new one. In Paper 2, both versions give you about a minute and a half per mark.

The value of each mark changes, though:

- **Paper 1:** one multiple choice mark used to be worth 1% of your final grade (30% spread over 30 marks). Now it is worth 0.75% (30% over 40 marks). One careless slip costs you a little less.
- **Paper 2:** one mark used to be worth about 0.78% of your grade (70% over 90 marks). Now it is worth 0.875% (70% over 80 marks). Every Paper 2 mark counts for more.
- **Section A** has shrunk from 30 marks to 20. It is now a quarter of Paper 2, so it deserves about 30 minutes, not 45.

The biggest single lesson: **Section B now carries three quarters of Paper 2**, so your choice of three questions out of four, and your part (d) answers, matter more than before.

## Less evaluation, more knowledge and analysis

This is the change most students miss. The assessment objectives have been reworded, and their weightings have moved.

| Assessment objective | Whole qualification, 2026 | Whole qualification, 2027 | Paper 2, 2026 | Paper 2, 2027 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| AO1 Knowledge and understanding | 40% | 43% | 35% | 40% |
| AO2 Analysis | 40% | 47% | 35% | 45% |
| AO3 Evaluation | 20% | 10% | 30% | 15% |

Paper 1 stays at 50% knowledge and 50% analysis, with no evaluation.

So evaluation has halved, from 20% to 10% of the qualification. It has not disappeared. The new syllabus says that in part (d) of each Section B question you must "evaluate economic issues" and develop "reasoned discussions that consider two sides of economic arguments". Those parts are marked with levels of response. Cambridge adds that, overall, the marking criteria stay largely the same.

What this means for your answers:

- **Definitions must be exact.** Knowledge is now 43% of the qualification. A loose definition of price elasticity of demand, or of a recession, costs a bigger share of your grade than it did.
- **Analysis is the largest objective.** Almost half of the qualification rewards you for explaining how one thing leads to another, with diagrams and data used correctly.
- **Evaluation is concentrated.** The syllabus singles out part (d) of each Section B question for two-sided discussion. When a question only asks you to explain, explain, and keep your "on the other hand" for the questions that ask for a judgement.

<Tip>
For every "explain" or "analyse" question, write a chain: the cause, then how it works, then the effect, then the effect on the person or group named in the question. A chain with three or four clear links is analysis. A list of points with no links between them is mostly knowledge.
</Tip>

## Content removed

Cambridge lists these as removed or significantly reduced. The topic numbers are from the **old** syllabus.

- Old 3.4 Trade unions (still mentioned in the new 3.3.2)
- Old 3.5.3 causes and forms of the growth of firms
- Old 4.1 The role of government
- Old 4.3.5 principles of taxation
- Old 4.7.2 changing patterns and level of employment
- The content on deflation in old 4.8
- Interpreting population pyramid diagrams, old 5.3.3
- Fixed foreign exchange rates, old 6.3.5, including the comparison of floating and fixed systems
- Mobility of capital, enterprise and land (mobility of labour stays, in 3.3.4)
- The claimant count as a measure of unemployment
- PPC references under causes of economic growth and under recession, and "changes in investment and technology" as a cause of growth

If your notes have long sections on deflation, fixed exchange rates or population pyramids, you can stop drilling them.

## Content added or made explicit

These are now examinable, or are spelled out more clearly than before:

- Environment and sustainability, built into topics across the syllabus (details below)
- The concept of scarcity
- The terms perfectly inelastic, unitary elastic and perfectly elastic, for both PED and PES
- Monopoly as a term linked to market failure, plus the non-provision of public goods and higher prices under monopoly as results of resource misallocation
- Arguments for and against the mixed economic system
- Age and culture as influences on household finances
- Drawing and interpreting diagrams of a national minimum wage
- Definitions of different types of mergers
- Average total cost diagrams showing economies and diseconomies of scale
- The effect of changes in investment on productivity
- Advantages and disadvantages of competitive markets, and the effect of having only one firm on price, quality, choice and profit
- Reasons for taxation
- Infrastructure spending as a supply-side policy
- Causes and consequences of recession
- Seasonal unemployment
- Improved healthcare provision as a policy to reduce poverty
- Causes and consequences of changes in globalisation
- Definitions of specialisation by country and of free trade
- Reasons for trade restrictions, and the advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade
- Reasons for buying and selling foreign currencies
- Definitions of floating exchange rate, appreciation and depreciation

### Where sustainability now appears

Cambridge says the themes of environment and sustainability "are now embedded within topics". In the new syllabus that means, for example:

- **Macroeconomic aims (4.1):** environmental sustainability is listed as a government aim, and economic growth versus environmental sustainability is listed as a possible conflict between aims.
- **Taxation (4.2.3):** encouraging environmental sustainability is one of the reasons for taxation.
- **Poverty (5.2.2):** environmental factors are listed as a cause of poverty.
- **Globalisation (6.2.2):** you need the effect of changes in globalisation on the environment.
- **Trade restrictions (6.2.5):** promoting environmental sustainability is one of the reasons for restricting trade.

Expect Section A data and Section B stems to use environmental contexts. The economics underneath is the same as always: costs, benefits, trade-offs, and who gains and who loses.

### The new globalisation topic

Topic 6.2.2 is new and worth learning carefully, because it gives you a ready-made answer structure. The causes of changes in globalisation are changes in trade restrictions, changes in transport costs, changes in communication costs, and the movement of multinational companies. The effects you need are on international trade, competition, the environment, migration, income distribution and economic development.

## What to do about it

**If you sit in 2027:**

1. Download the 2027 to 2029 syllabus from the 2281 page and use it as your checklist. Cross out the removed content above in your own notes, and add the new topics.
2. Practise with the new specimen papers. Cambridge updated the specimen Paper 2 mark schemes for questions 1(a), 3(b) and 5(a) in June 2025, so make sure your copy is the current one.
3. Keep using past papers: they are still the best practice you have. Skip multiple choice questions on removed topics, and when you practise an old Section A question, remember the new one is worth 20 marks, not 30.
4. Time yourself to the new papers: 1 hour for 40 multiple choice questions, and 2 hours for Paper 2.

**If you sit in 2028 or later:** do all of the above, and confirm with your school now that O Level 2281 will be offered in your zone.

**Checking where you stand:** the [grade thresholds](/grade-thresholds) page shows the real marks Cambridge required for each grade in recent sessions, and the [grade predictor](/tools/grade-predictor) turns your mock marks into a likely grade. Both are built on sessions taken under the old paper structure (30 and 90 marks), so for 2027 treat them as a guide, not a promise. Nobody knows the 2027 thresholds until Cambridge publishes them after the exams.
