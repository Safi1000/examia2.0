Paper 1 is 30% of your Economics grade, and it is the one paper where you cannot write your way to partial credit. Each question is right or wrong. The good news is that MCQs reward method more than memory. Students who lose marks here usually knew the economics. They misread a word, rushed a calculation or fell for an option that was nearly right. Below is the method I teach for each question type.

## Paper 1 facts (checked against the Cambridge syllabuses)

O Level 2281 and IGCSE 0455 have the same Paper 1 structure. The format changes for exams from 2027, so check which one applies to you.

| | Exams in 2026 | Exams from 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Questions | 30, answer all | 40, answer all |
| Time | 45 minutes | 1 hour |
| Marks | 30 | 40 |
| Weighting | 30% of the qualification | 30% of the qualification |
| Skills tested | AO1 knowledge and understanding, AO2 analysis (50% each) | AO1 and AO2 (50% each) |
| Calculator | Allowed | Allowed |

Three more facts from the paper itself:

- Each question has **four options, A, B, C and D**.
- **Each correct answer scores one mark.** A wrong answer scores zero, the same as a blank. So never leave a question blank.
- Questions come from **all six sections** of the syllabus (2026 syllabus), and they may include calculations and diagrams.

Paper 1 has no evaluation (AO3) marks. Every question tests whether you know a concept precisely and can apply it. That is why precise definitions matter so much here.

### Your pace

Both versions give you **90 seconds per question** (45 minutes for 30, or 60 minutes for 40). Many questions take 30 seconds. That leaves time for the diagrams and calculations, as long as you do not get stuck.

## A method for each question type

### 1. Calculation questions

Write the formula in the margin **before** you touch the numbers. Then calculate, and only then look at the options. The wrong options are usually the answers you get from a common slip, so if you look first, a wrong one will look familiar.

Typical slips the wrong options are built from:

- Dividing the wrong way round (price change over quantity change for elasticity).
- Using the change in units instead of the **percentage** change.
- Answering for one year when the question asks for several.

That last one is real. In the June 2024 O Level paper (2281/11), Question 2 asked for an opportunity cost over five years. The Cambridge examiner report says only 39 per cent chose the correct answer, and 41 per cent picked the one-year figure.

### 2. Diagram questions

Do not try to read a diagram in your head. Use the question paper:

1. Mark the **original** equilibrium.
2. Decide which curve moves, and draw the new one.
3. Mark the **new** equilibrium.
4. Only now read the options.

The trap is mixing an old point with a new curve. In the same June 2024 paper, Question 11 showed a minimum wage on a labour market diagram. The report says 27 per cent answered correctly. The two most popular wrong answers used the new curves but the original equilibrium point.

### 3. "NOT" and "most likely" questions

Words that change the meaning of a question are printed in bold. The June 2024 examiner report for 2281 tells candidates to read each item carefully, "taking note of any bold text".

For a **NOT** question, turn it into a true or false test. Write T or F next to A, B, C and D. Three will be true. The answer is the odd one out.

For a **most likely** question, follow the chain of cause and effect one step at a time, and write it down as arrows: interest rate falls → borrowing cheaper → spending rises. More than one option may be possible. You want the one that follows most directly from the change.

### 4. Definition questions

These look easy, so students answer them too fast. The wrong options are usually close to the right idea but miss one part of it. June 2024 Paper 11 opened with a simple question, "What is a free good?" The options included free samples of a new product and health services provided by the state, which cost the user nothing but still use scarce resources. The answer was sunlight. A free good has no opportunity cost, which is different from a good that costs the buyer nothing.

Learn each syllabus definition word for word. When two options look similar, ask which word makes one of them wrong.

## Practise on these (my own questions)

These are questions I wrote in the Paper 1 style. Try each one before reading the answer.

**Question A.** The price of a good rises from $4 to $5. Quantity demanded falls from 200 units to 170 units. What is the price elasticity of demand?

A −0.6  
B −1.67  
C −0.15  
D −30

**Answer: A.** Percentage change in quantity = −30 ÷ 200 × 100 = −15%. Percentage change in price = 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = 25%. PED = −15 ÷ 25 = −0.6. Option B divides the wrong way round. Option D uses the change in units (−30 ÷ 1).

**Question B.** Which of the following would **NOT** shift the demand curve for rice to the right?

A an increase in population  
B a rise in the price of wheat, a substitute  
C a fall in the price of rice  
D a successful advertising campaign for rice

**Answer: C.** A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve, not a shift. A, B and D are all true causes of a rightward shift, so C is the odd one out.

**Question C.** A government sets a maximum price for flour below the equilibrium price. At the maximum price, quantity demanded is 900 tonnes and quantity supplied is 600 tonnes. What is the result?

A a surplus of 300 tonnes  
B a shortage of 300 tonnes  
C a shortage of 900 tonnes  
D no change, because the maximum price is below equilibrium

**Answer: B.** Below equilibrium, demand is greater than supply, so there is a shortage equal to the gap: 900 − 600 = 300 tonnes. Option D mixes up the rule: a maximum price only has an effect when it is set **below** equilibrium.

**Question D.** What is opportunity cost?

A the money price paid for a product  
B the next best alternative forgone  
C the value of all the alternatives forgone  
D the cost of producing one more unit

**Answer: B.** Option C is the classic near miss. Opportunity cost is the single next best alternative, not every alternative.

**Question E.** A central bank lowers its rate of interest. What is most likely to happen?

A consumer borrowing falls  
B the exchange rate rises  
C total (aggregate) demand increases  
D the rate of inflation falls

**Answer: C.** Cheaper borrowing raises consumer spending and investment, so total demand rises. A lower interest rate usually makes the currency less attractive to savers, so B is the wrong direction. Higher demand tends to push inflation up, not down, so D is wrong too.

## The traps that catch good students

| Trap | What to do |
|---|---|
| Shift vs movement | A change in the good's own price is always a movement along the curve. |
| Percentage vs percentage points | Inflation rising from 2% to 4% is a rise of 2 percentage points, but a 100% increase in the rate. |
| GDP vs GDP per head | Living standards questions usually need **per head**. |
| Factor vs reward | Labour is a factor. Wages are its reward. Read which one the question asks for. |
| Absolute words | Options with "always" or "never" are often wrong, because economic effects depend on conditions. |

## A time plan you can follow

**Exams in 2026 (45 minutes, 30 questions)**

| Time | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0 to 30 minutes | First pass. Answer every question you can do quickly. Circle the hard ones and move on. |
| 30 to 40 minutes | Second pass on the circled questions. Use elimination. |
| 40 to 43 minutes | Make a final choice on anything still open. No blanks. |
| 43 to 45 minutes | Check the answer sheet: one mark per row, in the right row. |

**Exams from 2027 (1 hour, 40 questions)**

| Time | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0 to 40 minutes | First pass. Circle the hard ones. |
| 40 to 52 minutes | Second pass with elimination. |
| 52 to 57 minutes | Final choices. No blanks. |
| 57 to 60 minutes | Check the answer sheet. |

<Tip>
Shade the answer sheet as you go, not all at the end. One skipped row near the start can shift every answer after it by one line. Each time you circle and skip a question, check that your next answer goes into the next numbered row.
</Tip>

## How to practise

Do one past Paper 1 under timed conditions each week. When you mark it, sort every wrong answer into one of four boxes: did not know the content, misread the question, calculation slip, or fell for a near miss. After three papers, one box will be much bigger than the others. That tells you exactly what to fix next.
