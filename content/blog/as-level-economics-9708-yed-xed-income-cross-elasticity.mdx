Income elasticity of demand (YED) is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income. Cross elasticity of demand (XED) is the percentage change in quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good. For both, the sign is the answer: it tells you what type of good you have.

YED and XED are in section 2.2 of the [AS Level Economics 9708 syllabus](/syllabus/a-level-economics-9708#s-2.2). IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 do not include them: those syllabuses cover only price elasticity of demand (PED) and price elasticity of supply (PES).

## What are the PED, YED, XED and PES formulas?

| Measure | Formula | Usual sign |
|---|---|---|
| PED | % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price | Negative |
| YED | % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income | Positive or negative |
| XED | % change in quantity demanded of good A ÷ % change in price of good B | Positive or negative |
| PES | % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price | Positive |

Every percentage change is worked out the same way:

**percentage change = change ÷ original value × 100**

Always divide by the original value, the one before the change. The top line of every formula is a quantity. Only the bottom line changes: price, income or the price of another good. ## What does the YED value mean?

With PED you ignore the sign. With YED you must keep it, because the sign tells you whether the good is normal or inferior.

| YED | Type of good | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | Inferior good: people buy less as income rises | Cheap own brand food |
| Between 0 and 1 | Normal good, income inelastic (a necessity) | Basic foods such as rice |
| More than 1 | Normal good, income elastic (a luxury) | Foreign holidays, restaurant meals |

A **normal good** is any good with a positive YED. A **luxury** is a normal good too: it is the kind whose demand rises by a bigger percentage than income.

### Worked example: YED

A family's income rises from $500 to $550 a month. The number of restaurant meals it buys rises from 4 to 5 a month.

1. Change in income: $550 − $500 = $50. Percentage: 50 ÷ 500 × 100 = **+10%**.
2. Change in quantity: 5 − 4 = 1. Percentage: 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = **+25%**.
3. YED = 25 ÷ 10 = **+2.5**.

YED is positive, so restaurant meals are a normal good. It is more than 1, so demand is income elastic: for this family, restaurant meals are a luxury.

## What does the XED value mean?

XED tells you how two goods are related. Again, keep the sign.

| XED | The two goods are | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | Substitutes: one replaces the other | Tea and coffee |
| Negative | Complements: bought and used together | Printers and ink cartridges |
| Zero | Unrelated | Bread and phones |

The size matters too. A large positive XED means close substitutes, such as two brands of cola. A large negative XED means strong complements. A value near zero means a weak link.

### Worked example: XED

The price of coffee rises from $4.00 to $5.00 a packet. Sales of tea in the same shop rise from 2,000 to 2,300 packets a week.

1. Change in the price of coffee: $5.00 − $4.00 = $1.00. Percentage: 1.00 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = **+25%**.
2. Change in quantity of tea: 2,300 − 2,000 = 300. Percentage: 300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **+15%**.
3. XED = 15 ÷ 25 = **+0.6**.

XED is positive, so tea and coffee are substitutes. It is less than 1, so they are not very close substitutes: many coffee drinkers did not switch.

**Watch the order.** The quantity of the good you are asked about goes on top. The price of the other good goes underneath.

## How is price elasticity of supply different?

PES is section 2.3 of the 9708 syllabus, straight after YED and XED. It measures how quantity supplied responds to a change in the good's own price. PES is normally positive, because a higher price leads firms to supply more. Read it like PED: more than 1 is elastic, less than 1 is inelastic.

### Worked example: PES

The price of a chair rises from $10 to $12. A furniture maker raises the number it supplies from 1,000 to 1,300 a month.

1. Price: 2 ÷ 10 × 100 = **+20%**.
2. Quantity supplied: 300 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **+30%**.
3. PES = 30 ÷ 20 = **+1.5**, so supply is price elastic.

Supply tends to be more elastic when a firm has spare capacity, can store stocks, and has time to adjust. In the short run supply is often inelastic, and it becomes more elastic in the long run.

## How do firms and governments use YED and XED?

The 9708 syllabus asks for the implications of these values for decision making. In an essay, these are the uses that earn analysis marks.

- **Planning for the economic cycle.** When incomes rise, sales of luxuries rise faster than income, so a car maker or holiday firm can expect strong growth. In a recession the same firms lose sales fastest. Sellers of inferior goods may see sales rise in a recession.
- **Choosing what to produce.** A firm whose products are all income elastic is exposed to a downturn. Adding necessities spreads the risk.
- **Pricing against rivals.** If XED with a rival's product is high and positive, a price cut by the rival will take many customers. The firm has to watch rival prices closely.
- **Pricing complements.** With a strong negative XED, a firm can sell one good cheaply to raise demand for the other, such as a cheap printer and dearer ink cartridges.
- **Government planning.** As incomes grow, demand for income elastic services, such as air travel, grows faster than income, so a government may plan more capacity in advance.

## What mistakes do examiners report?

These come from the 9708 Principal Examiner Reports. Each one costs marks on Paper 1 or Paper 2.

| Mistake | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Using the percentage change in price to calculate YED (March 2026) | Divide by the percentage change in income |
| Defining elasticity in general without mentioning income (March 2026) | Name income in the YED definition and write the formula |
| Ignoring the sign of YED in a multiple choice question (March 2026) | Keep the sign: positive YED means sales fall when real income falls |
| Calling goods complements when one is a component of the other (March 2026) | Steel used to make cars is derived demand, not a complement |
| Writing the XED formula upside down, or without percentage changes (June 2023) | Quantity of good A on top, price of good B underneath, both as percentages |
| Unclear meaning of YED in a "PED or YED" essay (November 2023) | Define both, then apply each to the firm in the question |
| Writing about XED when the question asked only for PED and YED (November 2022) | Answer only the measures named in the question |
| Mixing up PES and PED (March 2022, June 2022, November 2023) | Supply means firms and quantity supplied; demand means buyers |

The March 2026 report put the multiple choice problem plainly: some candidates "ignored the positive sign for income elasticity of demand". That is an easy mark to lose.

## How are YED and XED questions marked?

YED and XED appear in both AS papers. Paper 1 is multiple choice. Paper 2 has a data response question in Section A and two part essays in Sections B and C, and it rewards knowledge, analysis and evaluation.

In a data response question, examiners look for three things: the formula, a correct calculation from the data, and the right classification of the good. The March 2026 report praised answers that calculated a positive YED and linked it to fast food being a normal good.

In an essay, examiners want the measure applied to the firm or market in the question. The November 2023 report criticised general answers that made little attempt at a judgement. So finish with a reasoned view, for example why YED may matter more than PED to a car maker when incomes are falling.

<Tip title="Write the sign and the type together">
Never give a YED or XED value without its sign and its meaning in the same sentence: "YED = −0.8, so this is an inferior good." The number alone only shows the calculation. Examiners also want the classification that follows from it.
</Tip>

## Can you answer these practice questions?

### Practice question 1

A worker's income falls from $800 to $720 a month. The number of bus journeys they make rises from 50 to 54 a month. Calculate YED and say what type of good bus travel is for this worker.

**Answer.** Income: −80 ÷ 800 × 100 = −10%. Quantity: +4 ÷ 50 × 100 = +8%. YED = 8 ÷ −10 = **−0.8**. YED is negative, so bus travel is an **inferior good** for this worker.

### Practice question 2

The price of printers falls from $100 to $80. Sales of ink cartridges rise from 1,000 to 1,100 a week. Calculate XED and say how the goods are related.

**Answer.** Price of printers: −20 ÷ 100 × 100 = −20%. Quantity of cartridges: +100 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = +10%. XED = 10 ÷ −20 = **−0.5**. XED is negative, so printers and ink cartridges are **complements**.

### Practice question 3

A good has a YED of +0.4. Real incomes rise by 5%. By how much does quantity demanded change, and what type of good is it?

**Answer.** Rearrange the formula: % change in quantity = YED × % change in income = 0.4 × 5 = **+2%**. YED is positive and less than 1, so it is a **normal good** that is income inelastic: a necessity.

## FAQ

### Is YED in IGCSE or O Level Economics?

No. IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 cover only price elasticity of demand and price elasticity of supply. YED and XED start at AS Level, in section 2.2 of the 9708 syllabus.

### Can YED be negative?

Yes. A negative YED means the good is inferior: people buy less of it as their income rises. A positive YED means a normal good.

### What does a YED greater than 1 mean?

Demand is income elastic: quantity demanded rises by a bigger percentage than income. The good is a normal good and a luxury.

### What does a negative XED mean?

The two goods are complements, used together. When the price of one rises, people buy less of the other.

### What does an XED of zero mean?

The goods are unrelated. A change in the price of one has no effect on the quantity demanded of the other.
