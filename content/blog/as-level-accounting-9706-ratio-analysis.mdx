AS Accounting 9706 lists twelve ratios in section 1.6.2: five for profitability, two for liquidity and five for efficiency. The syllabus prints a formula for each in its appendix and says these are the only formulas accepted. Learn them exactly, round days up to the next whole day, and when you evaluate, explain why each ratio moved before you give a decision.

## What does the 9706 syllabus ask for in ratio analysis?

Section 1.6, Analysis and communication of accounting information, has two parts.

| Syllabus section | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 1.6.1 Users of accounting information | Say what owners, managers, employees, investors, lenders, suppliers, customers, government, and public and environmental bodies each want to know |
| 1.6.2 Calculation and evaluation of ratios | Calculate the ratios, interpret them, suggest ways to improve them, and explain the limitations of accounting information |

Section 1.6.2 also expects you to use ratios to make business decisions. That is where the 5 mark advice questions come from.

On our past paper pages, 100 whole questions from the 9706 papers of 2022 to 2026 carry the 1.6 tag, as main or second tag: 89 in Paper 1 and 11 in Paper 2. In Paper 2, ratios came as a 15 mark Question 2 or Question 3 in every set I opened. You can work through them on the [9706 analysis and communication topic page](/past-papers/a-level-accounting-9706/topics/1-6-analysis-and-communication-of-accounting-information).

If you are new to ratios, start with my [IGCSE and O Level ratios guide](/blog/igcse-o-level-accounting-ratios-formulas) for the basics of each one. This article is about what 9706 adds and how AS Paper 2 marks it.

## Which ratio formulas does 9706 accept?

Here is every AS ratio as the appendix prints it. Learn the wording of each formula, because Paper 2 often asks you to state the formula before you calculate.

### Profitability ratios

| Ratio | Formula | Answer as |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| Mark-up | Gross profit ÷ cost of sales × 100 | % |
| Profit margin | Profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100 (the appendix adds a second version that labels this profit as after interest) | % |
| Return on capital employed (ROCE) | Profit from operations ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |
| Expenses to revenue | Expenses ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| Operating expenses to revenue | Operating expenses ÷ revenue × 100 | % |

The last two rows are one ratio, not two. Section 1.6.2 lists expenses to revenue with operating expenses to revenue in brackets beside it, and the appendix prints a formula for each version. That is why the count is five profitability ratios, not six.

Capital employed has its own definition in the appendix: issued shares plus reserves plus non-current liabilities.

### Liquidity ratios

| Ratio | Formula | Answer as |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | Ratio, such as 2.03 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | (Current assets minus inventory) ÷ current liabilities | Ratio, such as 1.09 : 1 |

### Efficiency ratios

| Ratio | Formula | Answer as |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current asset turnover | Net revenue ÷ total net book value of non-current assets | Times |
| Trade receivables turnover | Trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365 | Days |
| Trade payables turnover | Trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365 | Days |
| Inventory turnover | Average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 | Days |
| Rate of inventory turnover | Cost of sales ÷ average inventory | Times |

Gearing, interest cover, the working capital cycle, net working assets to revenue and the investment ratios are in the same appendix, but it marks them A Level only. You will not need them for Paper 1 or Paper 2.

### Why must you use the syllabus formula?

The syllabus is blunt about this. Section 1.6.2 and the Paper 2 description both say these are "the only formulae accepted in candidate responses". The assessment section adds two details. Ratios are not given on the question paper, so you must know them. And if you use a wrong formula, that ratio earns nothing, but the figure you get is still treated as your own figure in any later calculation.

So a student who works out receivables days with total revenue loses the marks for that ratio. If a later calculation uses that answer, the later calculation can still be marked on their figure.

### Do I use closing or average figures?

The appendix says to use year end balances where appropriate, unless the question asks for averages. The two inventory ratios are the exception: their formulas print average inventory. The schemes I opened follow this. In November 2025 Paper 21 and June 2026 Paper 22, average inventory was opening plus closing inventory, divided by two.

### How many decimal places, and do I round days up?

The appendix says to give the number of decimal places the question asks for. In the Paper 2 ratio questions I opened, every question that set a number asked for two.

Days are different. The March 2023 Paper 22 report said the receivables and payables days must always be "rounded UP to the next full day". None of the schemes I opened rounded a day down. March 2023 Paper 22 marked a payables working of 27.2 days as 28, and November 2025 Paper 21 marked an inventory turnover working of 35.1 days as 36.

<Tip title="Round days up, even from .1">
Write the division with its decimals first, then the whole number of days, rounded up. 39.61 days becomes 40 days, and 34.98 days becomes 35 days.
</Tip>

## Which figures go into each formula?

In the June 2022 and November 2022 Paper 22 reports, most candidates stated the right formula, but some then put the wrong figure into it. These are the figures to find first.

| Figure | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Profit from operations | Gross profit, plus other income, less operating expenses. It is before finance costs. If you only have profit for the year, add back the debenture or loan interest |
| Capital employed | Issued shares plus all reserves plus non-current liabilities. Current liabilities never go in |
| Credit sales | Revenue less cash sales. Questions often give a percentage of revenue |
| Credit purchases | Purchases less cash purchases. If purchases are missing, work them out: cost of sales plus closing inventory less opening inventory |
| Average inventory | (Opening inventory + closing inventory) ÷ 2 |
| Net book value of non-current assets | The total for all non-current assets, after depreciation |

Questions build the missing figures in on purpose. In November 2023 Paper 23 and June 2026 Paper 22, you had to work out cost of sales from a margin, then purchases, before the payables days.

## How do you calculate all twelve ratios from one set of accounts?

Here is my own example. Every figure has been checked.

> Larkspur Home Goods Limited sells furniture and household goods. Extracts for the year ended 31 March 2026 are below. Cash sales were 25% of revenue. Purchases of $32,000 were for cash; the rest were on credit. Finance costs are $9,000 of 6% debenture interest and $1,000 of overdraft interest.

| Statement of profit or loss extract | $ | $ |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | | 860,000 |
| Opening inventory | 58,000 | |
| Purchases | 512,000 | |
| | 570,000 | |
| Closing inventory | (66,000) | |
| Cost of sales | | (504,000) |
| Gross profit | | 356,000 |
| Operating expenses | | (228,000) |
| Profit from operations | | 128,000 |
| Finance costs | | (10,000) |
| Profit for the year | | 118,000 |

| Statement of financial position at 31 March 2026 | $ |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets at net book value | 410,000 |
| Inventory | 66,000 |
| Trade receivables | 70,000 |
| Other receivables | 4,000 |
| Cash at bank | 2,000 |
| Total current assets | 142,000 |
| Trade payables | 46,000 |
| Other payables | 6,000 |
| Bank overdraft | 18,000 |
| Total current liabilities | 70,000 |
| Ordinary share capital | 300,000 |
| Retained earnings | 32,000 |
| 6% debentures (2032) | 150,000 |

Check: total assets are 410,000 + 142,000 = 552,000. Equity and liabilities are 300,000 + 32,000 + 150,000 + 70,000 = 552,000.

### Step 1: the four figures you must build

- Credit sales = 75% × 860,000 = $645,000.
- Credit purchases = 512,000 less 32,000 = $480,000.
- Average inventory = (58,000 + 66,000) ÷ 2 = $62,000.
- Capital employed = 300,000 + 32,000 + 150,000 = $482,000.

### Step 2: the twelve ratios

| Ratio | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 356,000 ÷ 860,000 × 100 | 41.40% |
| Mark-up | 356,000 ÷ 504,000 × 100 | 70.63% |
| Profit margin | 118,000 ÷ 860,000 × 100 | 13.72% |
| ROCE | 128,000 ÷ 482,000 × 100 | 26.56% |
| Operating expenses to revenue | 228,000 ÷ 860,000 × 100 | 26.51% |
| Current ratio | 142,000 ÷ 70,000 | 2.03 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | (142,000 less 66,000) ÷ 70,000 = 76,000 ÷ 70,000 | 1.09 : 1 |
| Non-current asset turnover | 860,000 ÷ 410,000 | 2.10 times |
| Trade receivables turnover | 70,000 ÷ 645,000 × 365 = 39.61 | 40 days |
| Trade payables turnover | 46,000 ÷ 480,000 × 365 = 34.98 | 35 days |
| Inventory turnover | 62,000 ÷ 504,000 × 365 = 44.90 | 45 days |
| Rate of inventory turnover | 504,000 ÷ 62,000 | 8.13 times |

Notice three things. ROCE uses profit from operations, 128,000, not profit for the year. The overdraft sits in current liabilities, so it is not in capital employed. And this example has no separate "expenses" figure, so I used the operating expenses version. Use whichever version the question names.

### Step 3: compare with last year

The ratios only mean something next to a comparison. Here are last year's ratios, as a question would give them.

| Ratio | Year to 31 March 2025 | Year to 31 March 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 38.50% | 41.40% |
| Profit margin | 15.20% | 13.72% |
| Operating expenses to revenue | 21.80% | 26.51% |
| ROCE | 27.10% | 26.56% |
| Current ratio | 2.40 : 1 | 2.03 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 1.30 : 1 | 1.09 : 1 |
| Non-current asset turnover | 2.30 times | 2.10 times |
| Trade receivables turnover | 32 days | 40 days |
| Trade payables turnover | 41 days | 35 days |
| Inventory turnover | 38 days | 45 days |

## How do you evaluate ratios in a Paper 2 answer?

In my marking, this is where answers stall. A student calculates every ratio, then write "the current ratio has decreased, which is bad" and stop. That earns little.

The syllabus expects any recommendation or decision to be backed by reasons on both sides and then justified. The schemes I opened mark the 5 mark advice questions in a similar way. November 2023 Paper 23 and June 2025 Paper 21 gave up to 4 marks for comments and 1 for a decision backed by a comment. November 2022 Paper 22 also kept 1 mark for the decision, and June 2026 Paper 22 capped each side of the argument at 3.

### My method in four steps

This is how I teach it.

1. **State the movement with both figures.** "The acid test ratio fell from 1.30 : 1 to 1.09 : 1."
2. **Explain the cause through the numerator or the denominator.** Which figure moved? The November 2022 Paper 31 report (an A Level paper) said few candidates looked at both parts of each ratio, and marks for that discussion were low. The habit pays at AS too.
3. **Say what it means for this business.** Cash, debts, customers, suppliers.
4. **Weigh both sides, then decide.** On a liquidity advice question in June 2022 Paper 22 (Question 1(f)), the report said some candidates argued only for the proposal and so capped their own marks.

### Model answer

> The directors of Larkspur Home Goods want to refit the showroom for $40,000, paid through the bank overdraft. Use the ratios to advise them. [5]

**For the refit.** Gross profit margin rose from 38.50% to 41.40%, so each dollar of sales now earns more gross profit. A better showroom could raise sales and add to that. ROCE is still strong at 26.56%, only slightly below 27.10%, so the business uses its capital well.

**Against using the overdraft.** Liquidity is already weaker. The acid test ratio fell from 1.30 : 1 to 1.09 : 1, and the business already has an $18,000 overdraft. Customers now take 40 days to pay instead of 32, while suppliers are paid in 35 days instead of 41. Cash is going out faster than it comes in. Inventory is held for 45 days instead of 38, which ties up more cash.

The refit is a non-current asset, so current assets stay at $142,000 while current liabilities rise to $110,000. The current ratio would fall to 1.29 : 1 and the acid test to 0.69 : 1. The business might then struggle to pay its short term debts.

Profit margin also fell, from 15.20% to 13.72%, despite the better gross margin. Operating expenses rose from 21.80% to 26.51% of revenue, so cost control needs attention first.

**Decision.** The directors should not use the overdraft for the refit. They should fund it from a long term source, such as a debenture or a share issue, which leaves current liabilities unchanged. Before that, they should collect debts faster to bring receivables days back towards 32.

In my marking, each paragraph that links a ratio movement to its effect on the business would be one comment, and the decision with its reason the fifth mark. That is my judgement on my own question, not a Cambridge scheme.

### How can a business improve each ratio?

Section 1.6.2 lists possible measures to improve profitability, liquidity and efficiency. Ideas students find useful:

| Group | Ways to improve |
|---|---|
| Profitability | Raise selling prices where customers will accept it; buy more cheaply; cut operating expenses such as rent or advertising that brings no sales |
| Liquidity | Bring cash in from a long term source (owner's capital, a long term loan, a share issue); sell non-current assets the business no longer uses; hold less inventory |
| Efficiency | Chase credit customers and tighten credit terms; ask suppliers for longer credit; stock lines that sell quickly; use non-current assets more fully or sell idle ones |

Be careful with liquidity. In March 2023 Paper 22, the report found correct ways to raise the acid test were rare. Popular wrong ideas were higher prices and more sales, or extra credit sales with lower trade payables. The scheme for that paper accepted ideas such as the owner putting in more capital, borrowing long term, selling non-current assets, taking less cash in drawings or holding less inventory. The November 2025 Paper 21 scheme, on improving inventory days without simply cutting inventory, accepted stocking items in more demand, lower prices or discounts, and advertising.

### What are the limitations of ratio analysis?

Section 1.6.2 also covers the limitations of accounting information. The ones the schemes I opened credited, in my words:

- Businesses may use different accounting policies, such as depreciation methods, so their ratios cannot be compared fairly.
- Ratios use only money figures, so they say nothing about how skilled the staff are or how good the managers are.
- A fair comparison needs a business of roughly the same size that deals in the same kind of goods or services.
- The figures are historic. They show the past, and another business's accounts may be out of date by the time you see them.
- Figures can be made to look better at the year end.

In November 2022 Paper 22, the report said nearly every candidate did well on the part asking for two limitations. On the November 2023 Paper 23 advice question about comparing with other businesses, the report noted several good answers, with many explaining that only similar businesses should be compared.

## How do examiners test ratios in 9706 Paper 2?

Each Paper 2 ratio question I opened was worth 15 marks. The 2022 papers ran 1 hour 30 minutes; from March 2023 the paper was 1 hour 45 minutes. The question types repeat.

| Session and paper | What it asked | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| June 2022 Paper 22, Q3 | State the formula and calculate payables days, receivables days, ROCE and non-current asset turnover for a company; why that turnover matters to the directors; one reason shareholders read the accounts | 3, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2 |
| November 2022 Paper 22, Q3 | Formula and workings for two efficiency and two liquidity ratios; advise directors pleased with a profit rise; two limitations | 4, 4, 5, 2 |
| March 2023 Paper 22, Q3 | Acid test and both days ratios for a trader; why the acid test matters; two ways the owner could improve it; discuss the change in both days ratios against last year's given figures | 6, 2, 2, 5 |
| November 2023 Paper 23, Q2 | Receivables days; the formula for rate of inventory turnover, then closing inventory worked back from it; payables days; advice on comparing with other businesses | 3, 1, 3, 3, 5 |
| June 2025 Paper 21, Q3 | Formulas for gross profit margin and profit margin; two reasons the gross profit margin rose; two reasons the profit margin still fell; a liquidity view against averages for similar businesses; two other reasons comparisons with similar businesses are hard | 2, 2, 2, 5, 4 |
| November 2025 Paper 21, Q3 | Opening inventory and revenue worked out from gross profit margins, then closing inventory; inventory turnover in days for two years; two drawbacks of cutting inventory; two other ways to improve inventory days | 3, 2, 1, 5, 2, 2 |
| June 2026 Paper 22, Q2 | Cost of sales and purchases from a margin, payables days, a trading section with closing inventory found from the rate of inventory turnover, then advice on a proposal | 1, 2, 2, 5, 5 |

Two patterns stand out. First, the formula itself often carries a mark, so write it in words before the numbers. Second, working backwards from a ratio came up in four of the seven sets. June 2022 Paper 22 and November 2025 Paper 21 started from a gross profit margin. In November 2023 Paper 23 and June 2026 Paper 22, you found cost of sales from a margin, then used the rate of inventory turnover to find average inventory and closing inventory.

How the calculation marks split in the schemes I opened: in June 2022 Paper 22 and November 2023 Paper 23, a 3 mark ratio gave 1 for the formula, 1 for the correct figures and 1 for the answer. In November 2022 Paper 22 and June 2026 Paper 22, each ratio was worth 2: 1 for the formula and 1 for the answer. In March 2023 Paper 22, with no formula asked, each ratio had 1 mark for the figures and 1 for the answer.

## Where do students lose marks on ratios?

These come from the 9706 examiner reports. Each row uses the report's own strength of wording.

| What went wrong | Session and paper | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong profit in ROCE | June 2022 Paper 22: very few found the profit before interest. March 2022 Paper 12 Q21: most did not add back the debenture interest | Use profit from operations. If you only have profit for the year, add the interest back |
| Not knowing a formula | June 2022 Paper 22: on non-current asset turnover, very few scored at all, because they did not know the formula | Net revenue ÷ total net book value of non-current assets |
| Total sales instead of credit sales | November 2022 Paper 22: several used sales or revenue for receivables days, with similar slips for payables. June 2022 Paper 22: credit sales were often wrong, and some got credit purchases wrong | Take out cash sales and cash purchases first |
| Debentures due within the year left out of current liabilities | November 2022 Paper 22: several left the loan out of current liabilities. It was 6% debentures (2022) at a 31 August 2022 year end, which the scheme counted as current | Debentures or loans due for repayment within the year go in current liabilities |
| Ignoring the decimal places asked for | March 2023 Paper 22: less well prepared candidates did not give two decimal places | Two decimal places when asked. The same report also reminds you to round days up |
| Not knowing the rate of inventory turnover, or a wrong cost of sales | November 2023 Paper 23: results on stating the formula were very mixed. Working back to closing inventory was mixed too, with several not finding cost of sales correctly | Learn the formula. Find cost of sales from the margin first, then average inventory |
| Mixing up mark-up and margin | June 2022 Paper 11 Q8; November 2022 Paper 21 Q4(f), a price for a 20% profit margin; March 2026 Paper 12 Q22 and Q25 | Margin divides by revenue; mark-up divides by cost of sales |
| Undeveloped comments | March 2023 Paper 22: the acid test explanation often scored only 1 of its 2 marks; most comments on the days ratios were too thin for more than one or two marks, and many said the same thing twice | Each comment needs a ratio, a reason and an effect on the business |

A quick check for mark-up and margin: a mark-up of 40% is a margin of 40 ÷ 140, which is 28.57%. A margin is always the smaller of the two.

<Tip title="Write the formula in words first">
When a question says "state the formula used", write the syllabus formula in words, then the figures, then the answer. A correct formula can still earn its mark when an earlier figure is wrong.
</Tip>

## Who uses ratios, and what do they look for?

Section 1.6.1 lists the users. This is my summary of what each one cares about most.

| User | Main interest |
|---|---|
| Owners and investors | Profitability and ROCE, the return on their money and the safety of it |
| Managers | All three groups, to find what needs fixing |
| Employees | Profitability, as a sign of job security and pay |
| Lenders | Liquidity and profit, to judge whether interest and loans will be repaid |
| Suppliers | Liquidity and payables days, to judge whether to give credit |
| Customers | Whether the business will survive to supply goods and honour warranties |
| Government, public and environmental bodies | Profit for tax, and the business's effect on jobs and the local area |

## Practice questions with worked answers

All three are my own, with my own figures.

### Question 1: build the figures, then the days

Saffron Kitchenware had revenue of $460,000, of which 15% was cash sales. It sells at a gross profit margin of 30%. Inventory was $27,000 at the start of the year and $33,000 at the end. All purchases were on credit. At the year end, trade receivables were $31,500 and trade payables were $23,400.

Calculate trade receivables turnover, trade payables turnover and inventory turnover, all in days.

**Answer.**
- Credit sales = 85% × 460,000 = $391,000. Receivables days = 31,500 ÷ 391,000 × 365 = 29.41, so **30 days**.
- Cost of sales = 70% × 460,000 = $322,000. Purchases = 322,000 + 33,000 less 27,000 = $328,000. Payables days = 23,400 ÷ 328,000 × 365 = 26.04, so **27 days**.
- Average inventory = (27,000 + 33,000) ÷ 2 = $30,000. Inventory days = 30,000 ÷ 322,000 × 365 = 34.01, so **35 days**.

### Question 2: work backwards from the rate of inventory turnover

A trader's cost of sales was $266,000. Goods are sold at a mark-up of 40%. The rate of inventory turnover was 7 times. Opening inventory was $36,500.

(a) Calculate revenue and the gross profit margin.
(b) Calculate closing inventory.

**Answer.**
(a) Revenue = 266,000 × 140 ÷ 100 = **$372,400**. Gross profit = 372,400 less 266,000 = $106,400. Gross profit margin = 106,400 ÷ 372,400 × 100 = **28.57%**.
(b) Average inventory = 266,000 ÷ 7 = $38,000. Opening plus closing inventory = 38,000 × 2 = 76,000. Closing inventory = 76,000 less 36,500 = **$39,500**.

### Question 3: the wrong formula and your own figure

A company's profit from operations was $54,000. It paid $4,000 of interest on 8% debentures of $50,000, so profit for the year was $50,000. Equity was $300,000. A student wrote ROCE = 50,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = 16.67%. The next part asked how many percentage points the company's ROCE was above a rival's 15.00%, and the student wrote 16.67 less 15.00 = 1.67 points.

(a) Calculate the correct ROCE.
(b) Explain what the student loses and what they can keep.

**Answer.**
(a) Capital employed = 300,000 + 50,000 = $350,000. ROCE = 54,000 ÷ 350,000 × 100 = **15.43%**.
(b) The student used profit after interest and left the debentures out of capital employed. That is not the syllabus formula, so the ROCE itself earns nothing. The syllabus says the resulting figure is credited as their own figure when it is used in following calculations, so the 1.67 points working can still earn its marks. The correct gap is 15.43 less 15.00 = 0.43 points.

## FAQ

### Can I use a different ratio formula if it gives the same answer?

No. The 9706 syllabus says only its appendix formulas are accepted. A wrong formula earns no marks for that ratio, though your figure can carry forward as your own figure in later calculations.

### Are the ratio formulas given in the exam?

No. The syllabus says ratios are not given on the question paper, so learn all twelve AS formulas by heart.

### Do I round days ratios up or to the nearest day?

Up, to the next whole day. The March 2023 Paper 22 report said so for receivables and payables days, and the November 2025 Paper 21 scheme rounded an inventory working of 35.1 days up to 36.

### Do I use average inventory or closing inventory?

Average inventory for inventory turnover in days and the rate of inventory turnover, because the appendix formulas say so. For other ratios, use year end figures unless the question asks for averages.

### What is capital employed in 9706 ROCE?

Issued shares plus reserves plus non-current liabilities. The profit to use is profit from operations, which is before finance costs.

### Which ratios are A Level only?

Gearing, interest cover, the working capital cycle, net working assets to revenue, and the investment ratios such as earnings per share. The appendix marks them A Level only.
