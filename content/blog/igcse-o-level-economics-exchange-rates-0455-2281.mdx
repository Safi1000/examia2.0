A foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, such as $1 = 280 rupees. Under a floating exchange rate, that price is set by the demand for and supply of the currency. When the currency rises in value it has **appreciated**; when it falls it has **depreciated**. A depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. In IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 this is syllabus section 6.3.

## What does the 2027 syllabus include on exchange rates?

The 0455 and 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029 have the same section 6.3. Here it is as an exam checklist, in my words.

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 6.3.1 Definition | Define a foreign exchange rate |
| 6.3.2 Reasons for buying and selling currencies | Explain the six reasons listed: trade in goods and services; speculation; government intervention; payment of profit, interest and dividends; workers' remittances; investment in capital goods |
| 6.3.3 How the rate is set | Define floating rate, appreciation and depreciation; use demand and supply of a currency; explain causes of changes |
| 6.3.4 Consequences | Explain the effects on the prices of exports and imports and on demand for them |

The syllabus names three causes of changes in the rate: changes in demand for exports and imports, changes in the interest rate, and speculation.

### What was removed and added for 2027?

The changes page of both syllabuses lists old topic 6.3.5, fixed foreign exchange rates, under content "removed or significantly reduced". It also says the comparison of floating and fixed systems, with their advantages and disadvantages, was removed. The reasons for buying and selling currencies, and the definitions of floating rate, appreciation and depreciation, were added.

This matters for practice. The March 2026 0455 Paper 22 still asked about a fixed exchange rate, and so did the 0455 November 2022 Paper 21. Those questions were set on the syllabus before 2027. Use them for the effects of a rate change, but do not learn the fixed versus floating debate for a 2027 exam.

On our past paper pages, 81 questions from the 0455 and 2281 papers of 2022 to 2026 carry the 6.3 tag, as main or second tag (0455: 51, 2281: 30).

## What is a foreign exchange rate, exactly?

For the 2 mark definition in the June 2022 Paper 21 mark scheme, one mark is for the price or value of a currency and one for "in terms of another currency". The report for that paper said weaker answers mentioned the comparison with another currency but left out price or value. The weakest confused the rate with the balance between exports and imports.

So write: **a foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another currency.** Add an example if you like, such as $1 = 280 rupees.

The other three terms:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Floating exchange rate | A rate set by the market forces of demand for and supply of the currency |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of a floating currency: it buys more of other currencies |
| Depreciation | A fall in the value of a floating currency: it buys less of other currencies |

**Read the direction carefully.** If the rate moves from $1 = 250 rupees to $1 = 280 rupees, each dollar buys more rupees. The dollar has appreciated and the rupee has depreciated. The June 2022 Paper 22 report said a number of candidates confused a depreciation with an appreciation, and the June 2023 Paper 22 report said some confused a rise in the rate with a fall.

## Why do people buy and sell foreign currencies?

Each of the six reasons in 6.3.2 creates demand for one currency and supply of another. When a Pakistani firm buys from the USA, it supplies rupees and demands dollars.

| Reason | Example of demand for rupees |
|---|---|
| Trade in goods and services | A US shop buys Pakistani footballs, or a tourist visits Lahore |
| Speculation | Traders buy rupees because they expect the rupee to rise |
| Government intervention | The central bank buys rupees with its foreign currency reserves |
| Profit, interest and dividends | A foreign firm pays a Pakistani shareholder |
| Workers' remittances | A worker in Dubai sends wages home to family |
| Investment in capital goods | A foreign company builds a factory in Pakistan |

The March 2025 0455 Paper 22 asked about causes of a rise in demand for foreign currency. The report said that, after past sessions where candidates struggled with exchange rate questions, there were many good answers, including on interest rates and foreign direct investment.

## How is a floating exchange rate determined?

Draw it like any market. Picture a diagram with the price of the rupee in dollars on the vertical axis and the quantity of rupees on the horizontal axis.

- The **demand curve** for rupees slopes down. It comes from foreigners buying Pakistani exports and assets.
- The **supply curve** of rupees slopes up. It comes from Pakistanis buying imports and foreign assets.
- The **equilibrium exchange rate** is where the two cross.

A shift in either curve changes the rate.

| Change | Curve that shifts | Effect on the rupee |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign demand for Pakistani exports rises | Demand for rupees shifts right | Appreciates |
| Pakistanis buy more imports | Supply of rupees shifts right | Depreciates |
| Pakistan's interest rate rises | Demand for rupees shifts right (hot money flows in) | Appreciates |
| Speculators expect the rupee to fall | Supply of rupees shifts right as they sell | Depreciates |
| Central bank buys rupees with its reserves | Demand for rupees shifts right | Appreciates |

The interest rate link: a higher interest rate attracts savings from abroad, sometimes called **hot money**. To deposit in Pakistan, foreigners must buy rupees. The June 2023 Paper 22 report praised answers that explained hot money flows.

Draw the diagram whenever you explain a cause in a longer answer. The syllabus says some optional Section B questions may include drawing diagrams. Label both axes, both curves, the old and new rates, and show the arrow of the shift.

### What causes a currency to depreciate?

These are causes the June 2022 Paper 22 mark scheme accepted for a depreciation, in my words:

1. Higher inflation or lower productivity at home, so exports become less competitive.
2. Higher incomes at home, so people buy more imports.
3. Lower incomes abroad, so foreigners buy fewer exports.
4. A fall in the interest rate, so money flows out.
5. Speculation that the currency will fall.
6. Less foreign direct investment coming in.

Each one works through either less demand for the currency or more supply of it. The June 2023 Paper 22 scheme lists the mirror image for an appreciation.

## What happens to export and import prices when the rate changes?

This is the effect most questions build on. Here is my own example, with hypothetical figures, not real data.

> A Sialkot firm sells footballs for 2,800 rupees each. It also imports a machine part priced at $50. The exchange rate moves from $1 = 250 rupees to $1 = 280 rupees.

**Export price in dollars.**
Before: 2,800 ÷ 250 = $11.20. After: 2,800 ÷ 280 = $10.00.
The footballs are $1.20 cheaper for a US buyer, even though the firm still gets 2,800 rupees.

**Import price in rupees.**
Before: 50 × 250 = 12,500 rupees. After: 50 × 280 = 14,000 rupees.
The machine part costs 1,500 rupees more.

So when the rupee **depreciates**: exports become cheaper abroad and imports become dearer at home. When it **appreciates**, the opposite happens.

<Tip title="Check the direction with one number">
Before writing about effects, put a price through the old and new rate, as above. It takes ten seconds and stops you writing that a rise in the rate makes exports cheaper.
</Tip>

### Does a cheaper export price always mean more export revenue?

No. Export revenue rises only if foreign demand is price elastic, so the rise in quantity sold outweighs the lower price. The March 2022 Paper 22 mark scheme credited "if demand is price elastic" in the chain from a fall in the rate to a smaller current account deficit. The June 2023 Paper 21 report added that a rise in the rate lowers export revenue unless exports are inelastic in demand.

This is your evaluation point in nearly every 8 mark question on exchange rates.

## How does an exchange rate change affect the economy?

A change in the rate works through export and import prices to four macroeconomic aims. Here are the main chains for a **depreciation**. The first three rows are built from the March 2022 Paper 22 and November 2022 Paper 22 mark schemes. The last row follows the 2027 specimen Paper 2 scheme, which lists more growth and more employment among the reasons a government may want a lower rate.

| Aim | Chain for a depreciation |
|---|---|
| Current account | Exports cheaper, imports dearer, so export revenue may rise and import spending fall, if demand is price elastic. A deficit may shrink. |
| Inflation (cost push) | Imported raw materials cost more, so firms' costs rise and prices rise. |
| Inflation (demand pull) | More exports and fewer imports raise total demand. |
| Growth and jobs | Higher net exports raise output, so firms may hire more workers. |

For an **appreciation**, reverse every chain: inflation may fall, but exporters may lose sales and jobs, and a current account deficit may grow. These are the points the June 2023 Paper 21 mark scheme listed on each side for whether a rise in the rate benefits an economy.

The November 2023 Paper 23 report for 2281 is worth reading on this. The question asked how an appreciation could reduce inflation. The report said fewer candidates could explain the link through less demand for exports and cheaper imports. Some wrote that higher demand for cheaper exports would raise total demand, which is the effect of a depreciation, not an appreciation.

In my teaching, this is where students lose the thread: they know the price effect, then write a chain that belongs to the opposite change. Writing "appreciation" or "depreciation" at the start of every line keeps you honest.

## How are exchange rate questions marked?

The 2027 Paper 2 is 2 hours and 80 marks. Section A is one compulsory 20 mark question. In Section B you answer three of four questions, each worth 20 marks in four parts. In the specimen Paper 2, those parts are worth 2, 4, 6 and 8 marks. Specimen Paper 2 question 5(a) asked for two reasons a government may want a lower exchange rate, for 1 mark each.

Here is how the old papers marked each type on exchange rates.

| Question type and marks | Paper (0455 and 2281 where shared) | How marks were given |
|---|---|---|
| Define foreign exchange rate [2] | June 2022 Paper 21 | 1 for price or value, 1 for in terms of another currency |
| Explain two causes of a change [4] | June 2022 Paper 22, June 2023 Paper 22 | 1 for each cause, 1 for each development of it |
| Analyse how a change affects inflation or the current account [6] | March 2022 Paper 22 (0455), November 2022 Paper 22 | 1 mark for each link in the chain |
| Discuss whether a change benefits an economy [8] | June 2023 Paper 21 | Levels: both sides developed for the top level |

For the 8 mark discuss question, the specimen Table A gives 6 to 8 marks for a reasoned discussion of two sides, 3 to 5 where it may be one sided, and 1 to 2 for undeveloped statements. A good 8 mark answer explains at least one benefit and one cost, each as a chain, then judges which matters more for the country in the question.

The June 2025 Paper 21 report gives a warning. It asked whether a floating exchange rate benefits an economy. Many answers only compared a strong and a weak currency, without discussing the floating system itself. The best answers recognised that a floating rate can remove a current account imbalance. A more common point was the uncertainty a changing rate creates for trade and investment.

## What mistakes do examiners report?

| Mistake (session and paper) | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Confusing a rise in the rate with a fall (June 2022 P22, June 2023 P22) | Check the direction with a price, as in the football example |
| Writing about the internal value of money (inflation) instead of the external value (June 2022 P22) | The exchange rate is the external value, against other currencies |
| Saying a rise in the rate makes exports cheaper and imports dearer (June 2023 P21) | A rise makes exports dearer and imports cheaper |
| Defining the rate as the balance of exports and imports (June 2022 P21, the weakest answers) | Price of one currency in terms of another |
| Unexplained statements, such as that a bigger money supply lowers the rate (some, 0455 March 2025 P22, in a money supply question) | Give the reason for each step |
| Saying "imported inflation" without the chain (0455 November 2022 P22) | Imports dearer, costs rise, prices rise |
| A floating rate question answered as strong versus weak currency (0455 June 2025 P21) | Discuss the system: automatic adjustment against uncertainty |
| Thinking a devaluation would reduce a current account surplus (0455 March 2025 P12, 23% of candidates) | A lower rate raises net exports, so a surplus grows |

## Practice questions with answers

All three are my own, with hypothetical figures.

### Practice question 1

> An Indian clothing firm sells a shirt for 1,200 rupees. The exchange rate changes from $1 = 80 rupees to $1 = 75 rupees.
>
> (a) Calculate the price of the shirt in dollars before and after the change. [2]
>
> (b) State whether the rupee has appreciated or depreciated. [1]

**Answer**

(a) Before: 1,200 ÷ 80 = $15. After: 1,200 ÷ 75 = $16.

(b) The rupee has appreciated: a dollar now buys fewer rupees, so each rupee is worth more.

### Practice question 2

> A central bank raises its interest rate from 4% to 7%. Explain how this could affect the country's foreign exchange rate. [4]

**Answer**

A higher interest rate gives a better return on savings held in the country **[1]**. Foreign savers move funds there, so demand for the currency rises **[1]**. With a floating rate, the demand curve shifts right and the rate appreciates **[1]**. Speculators who expect the rise may also buy the currency, adding to demand **[1]**.

How I would mark it is my own judgement, not a Cambridge scheme: one mark for each linked step.

### Practice question 3

> Analyse how an appreciation of its currency could affect a country's tourism industry. [6]

**Answer**

An appreciation means the currency buys more foreign currency **[1]**. Foreign tourists must now pay more of their own money for each unit of the country's currency **[1]**, so hotel rooms and meals cost them more **[1]**. If their demand is price elastic, fewer tourists visit **[1]**. Hotels earn less revenue and may cut staff **[1]**. At the same time, local people find holidays abroad cheaper, so some spend abroad instead of at home **[1]**.

Again, this marking is my own, one mark per link, in the style of the 6 mark schemes I have named.

## FAQ

### What is the definition of a foreign exchange rate?

The price of one currency in terms of another currency. For example, $1 = 280 rupees.

### What is the difference between appreciation and depreciation?

Appreciation is a rise in the value of a floating currency against others. Depreciation is a fall. A depreciated currency buys less foreign currency.

### Are fixed exchange rates in the 2027 syllabus?

No. The 0455 and 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029 list old topic 6.3.5, fixed foreign exchange rates, as removed or significantly reduced. Only floating rates are named in 6.3.3.

### How does an interest rate rise affect the exchange rate?

It attracts savings from abroad, so demand for the currency rises and a floating rate appreciates.

### Does a depreciation always improve the current account?

Not always. Export revenue rises and import spending falls only if demand for them is price elastic. Higher import costs can also raise inflation.

### What does SPICED mean?

It is a memory aid I give my students: Strong currency, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer. A weak currency does the opposite.
