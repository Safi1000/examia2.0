A market fails when, left alone, it produces the wrong amount of something: too much of a good that harms people, too little of one that helps them, or none at all of something everyone needs. The 0455 syllabus covers what market failure is, its causes and its consequences, with public goods, merit and demerit goods, and private, external and social costs and benefits as the key terms.

Most of it comes down to one idea: the buyer and the seller only count their own costs and benefits, not everyone else's.

## Costs and benefits: private, external, social

| Term | Who it falls on | Example: a factory making cement |
|---|---|---|
| Private cost | The producer or consumer making the decision | Wages, raw materials, electricity |
| External cost | Third parties, not paid for by the decision maker | Dust that damages the health of people living nearby |
| Social cost | Everyone | Private cost + external cost |

Benefits work the same way:

**social benefit = private benefit + external benefit**

A student who stays in education gets a private benefit (higher future earnings). Others gain an external benefit (a more skilled workforce, less unemployment).

### With numbers

Each tonne of cement costs the factory $50 to make. Each tonne also causes $20 of health and cleaning costs to people nearby.

- Private cost: **$50** a tonne.
- External cost: **$20** a tonne.
- Social cost: $50 + $20 = **$70** a tonne.

The factory sets its price and output using the $50, because the $20 is not its bill. So cement is cheaper than its true cost to society, and more of it is produced and bought than is best for society. That is market failure: **over-production** because of an external cost.

## Merit goods and demerit goods

| | Merit good | Demerit good |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A good that benefits the consumer more than they realise, and usually benefits others too | A good that harms the consumer more than they realise, and usually harms others too |
| Examples | Education, vaccinations, healthcare | Cigarettes, alcohol, sugary drinks |
| What the market does | Under-provides it: people buy too little | Over-provides it: people buy too much |
| Why | Consumers underestimate the benefit, and ignore the external benefit | Consumers underestimate the harm, and ignore the external cost |

The word that matters in both is **information**. People make the "wrong" choice because they do not have, or do not act on, full information about the good.

## Public goods

A public good has two features:

- **Non-excludable.** Once it is provided, nobody can be stopped from using it.
- **Non-rival.** One person using it does not reduce what is left for others.

Street lighting and national defence are the standard examples. Because nobody can be made to pay, people wait for someone else to pay (the **free rider** problem). No private firm can make a profit, so the market provides **none at all**, even though everyone values it. That is the most extreme kind of market failure.

<Tip>
Check both features before calling something a public good. A crowded motorway is rival (one more car slows everyone down), and a toll road is excludable. Neither is a pure public good, even though both are often provided by governments.
</Tip>

## What governments can do

| Problem | Policies | How they help |
|---|---|---|
| External costs, demerit goods | Indirect tax, regulation, bans, information campaigns | Raise the price or limit the quantity, so less is produced and consumed |
| External benefits, merit goods | Subsidies, state provision, information campaigns | Lower the price or provide it directly, so more is consumed |
| Public goods | State provision, paid for by taxation | The government provides what the market will not |

A tax on cement equal to the $20 external cost would make the producer pay the full $70 social cost. Price would rise and output would fall towards the level that is best for society.

## Building an 8-mark discuss answer

Take a typical question: *Discuss whether a government should tax sugary drinks.*

1. **Why there is a problem.** Sugary drinks are a demerit good. Consumers underestimate the harm to their health, and there are external costs, such as higher spending on public healthcare.
2. **Why a tax could work.** A tax raises the price, so quantity demanded falls and consumption moves closer to the socially best level. The tax revenue could pay for healthcare or for information campaigns.
3. **Why it might not.** If demand is price inelastic, consumption falls only a little. The tax takes a bigger share of the income of poorer households. People may switch to untaxed sugary foods.
4. **A judgement.** Say which side is stronger and what it depends on. "A tax is likely to reduce consumption only a little, because demand for sugary drinks is fairly inelastic, so it works best alongside education campaigns that tackle the information problem directly."

Two developed sides with no decision cannot reach the top level. The full method for discuss questions, with the mark bands, is in my [guide to answering Paper 2 questions](/blog/how-to-answer-igcse-o-level-economics-questions).

The link between elasticity and how well a tax works is set out with numbers in my [price elasticity of demand article](/blog/igcse-economics-price-elasticity-of-demand).

## Quick check

A power station produces electricity at a private cost of $30 per unit. Its pollution causes an external cost of $12 per unit. What is the social cost, and will the market produce too much or too little electricity?

**Answer.** Social cost = $30 + $12 = **$42** per unit. The power station prices on the $30 private cost, so electricity is cheaper than its full cost to society, and the market produces **too much**.
