Wages are set by the demand for labour and the supply of labour. A job pays well when firms want many of those workers and few people can do it, for example jobs that need long training. Trade unions, a national minimum wage and discrimination also change wages. For IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281, this is section 3.3, Workers.

## What does the syllabus say about workers and wages?

Section 3.3 has the same five parts in the [IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus](/syllabus/igcse-economics-0455) and the [O Level Economics 2281 syllabus](/syllabus/o-level-economics-2281) for 2027 to 2029.

| Part | What you need to know |
|---|---|
| 3.3.1 Choice of occupation | Wage and non-wage factors |
| 3.3.2 Wage determination | Demand for and supply of labour, trade unions, government policy including the national minimum wage, and labour market diagrams |
| 3.3.3 Differences in wages | Why wages differ, and how that depends on skill, sector, discrimination and private or public sector |
| 3.3.4 Mobility of labour | Causes and consequences of changes in occupational and geographical mobility |
| 3.3.5 Division of labour | Its meaning (worker specialisation), advantages and disadvantages |

Two notes from the syllabus changes page. Trade unions were a separate topic in the old syllabus. The changes page puts that old topic under content removed or significantly reduced, but notes that trade unions are still referenced in 3.3.2. So you still need them inside wage determination. The changes page also lists drawing and interpreting national minimum wage diagrams as added content.

Specialisation by **country** is a different topic, 6.1 in the same syllabus. In this article, specialisation means division of labour between workers.

## What affects a person's choice of job?

People compare two kinds of reward. The **wage** is the money paid for the work. **Non-wage factors** are everything else about the job that makes it more or less attractive.

The November 2025 0455 Paper 21 mark scheme accepted a long list of non-wage factors. Here are some of them, grouped my way:

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Security and benefits | Job security, fringe benefits such as a company car, paid holidays |
| Time | Working hours, flexibility, working from home |
| Place | Location, transport to work, being near family |
| Recognition | Being valued and recognised at work |

A low paid job can still attract people if the non-wage factors are strong. That is why a wage alone never fully explains a person's choice.

## How are wages determined?

Labour is bought and sold in a market, just like a product. Firms demand labour. Workers supply it. The wage is the price of labour.

### What affects the demand for labour?

The demand for labour is **derived demand**: firms want workers because people want the product those workers make. If demand for food rises, farmers want more farm workers.

Mark schemes have rewarded these influences on demand (November 2023 0455 Paper 22 and November 2024 0455 Paper 21):

- **Demand for the product.** More sales mean more workers are needed.
- **The wage rate.** The lower the wage, the cheaper it is to employ each worker.
- **Productivity.** More skilled workers produce more, so each one is worth more to the firm.
- **The price of other factors.** If machines become cheaper, firms may replace workers with them. If machines become dearer, demand for workers may rise.

### What affects the supply of labour?

Supply is the number of people willing and able to do a job at each wage. From the November 2024 0455 Paper 21 scheme, the November 2022 0455 Paper 22 scheme and the 2027 specimen mark scheme:

- **The size of the labour force.** Fewer people of working age means a smaller supply.
- **Qualifications and training.** Long or costly training reduces the number who can do the job.
- **Wages in other jobs.** If other occupations pay more, people move to them.
- **Non-wage factors.** Poor working conditions reduce supply.

### What does the labour market diagram look like?

I cannot draw it here, so build it from these words. Put the **wage rate** on the vertical axis and the **quantity of labour** on the horizontal axis. The March 2023 0455 Paper 12 report described labour supply graphs set out this way.

- The demand for labour curve slopes **down** from left to right: at a lower wage, firms employ more workers.
- The supply of labour curve slopes **up**: at a higher wage, more people want the job.
- Where they cross is the **equilibrium wage** and the number employed.

| Change | Curve that shifts | Effect on wage | Effect on number employed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand for the product rises | Demand right | Rises | Rises |
| Machines replace workers | Demand left | Falls | Falls |
| Training becomes longer | Supply left | Rises | Falls |
| More people gain the skill | Supply right | Falls | Rises |

The 2027 specimen mark scheme gave marks on a product market diagram for labelled axes, labelled original curves, the shifted curve and both equilibrium points. My advice is to use the same checklist for every labour market diagram.

### Worked example: a labour market with a minimum wage

All figures here are my own. This is the market for warehouse packers in one town.

| Wage per hour ($) | Packers demanded | Packers supplied |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 900 | 300 |
| 3 | 800 | 500 |
| 4 | 700 | 700 |
| 5 | 600 | 900 |
| 6 | 500 | 1 100 |

**Step 1: find the equilibrium.** Demand equals supply at $4 an hour, with 700 packers employed.

**Step 2: a national minimum wage of $5.** Firms may not pay less than $5. At $5, firms want only 600 packers, but 900 people want the work.

| At the $5 minimum wage | Packers |
|---|---:|
| People who want to work | 900 |
| Jobs firms offer | 600 |
| Surplus of labour (unemployed) | 300 |

The 300 come from two places. Employment falls from 700 to 600, so 100 packers lose their jobs. The higher wage also attracts 200 extra people (900 minus 700) who cannot find a packing job. On the diagram, the minimum wage is a horizontal line above the equilibrium. The gap between the two curves along that line is the surplus.

**Step 3: a minimum wage of $3 instead.** This is below the $4 equilibrium. The market already pays more, so the minimum wage has no effect. The June 2024 0455 Paper 22 mark scheme gave a mark for exactly this point.

**Step 4: demand for packers rises.** Online shopping grows, so firms want 300 more packers at every wage. New demand is 1 200, 1 100, 1 000, 900 and 800 at wages of $2 to $6. Demand now equals supply at $5, with 900 employed. The $5 minimum wage now matches the equilibrium, so it no longer causes a surplus.

### How does a national minimum wage work?

A national minimum wage is a legal lower limit on what employers may pay. The June 2024 0455 Paper 22 scheme accepted calling it a wage floor.

Its effects can go both ways, which is why it suits an 8 mark discuss question.

| It may help | It may harm |
|---|---|
| Low paid workers can afford more necessities, reducing poverty | If set above equilibrium, supply of labour exceeds demand, so unemployment rises |
| Higher pay may raise productivity | Firms' costs rise, so they may cut output or replace workers with machines |
| More spending raises total demand, so firms may employ more | It may add to inflation |
| A stronger incentive to work | Workers in informal jobs may not be covered |

These points come from the March 2022 and June 2024 0455 Paper 22 mark schemes and the June 2025 0455 Paper 21 report. The informal jobs point is from a strong candidate answer printed in the March 2022 report, not from the examiners' own comments. The same report noted that some candidates stopped at "higher wages and more spending". They needed to carry the chain on to total demand, employment and then poverty.

## How do trade unions affect wages?

A trade union is an organisation of workers that bargains with employers on their behalf. That is my wording; I have not found a definition in the mark schemes I checked. Bargaining as a group gives workers more **bargaining power** than each one would have alone.

### Why do workers join a trade union?

The June 2022 Paper 21 mark scheme (0455) rewarded these reasons:

- Greater bargaining power to negotiate higher wages, better working conditions and fringe benefits.
- Someone to speak for them in a dispute with the employer, for example if they are dismissed unfairly.
- Influence on government, for example to raise the national minimum wage.
- Member services, such as training courses or help with legal and money matters.

That scheme also gave no marks for examples of industrial action.

### Why do some workers not join?

The March 2023 0455 Paper 22 scheme accepted reasons like these:

- They cannot afford the membership fee.
- The law limits unions, or the government discourages them, so membership brings less benefit.
- Employers discourage membership, and workers fear for their jobs or promotion.
- They are already happy with their pay and conditions.
- They do not want to strike and lose pay.

### Do trade unions benefit workers?

| Why they might | Why they might not |
|---|---|
| Collective bargaining can win higher wages | Higher wages raise firms' costs, so firms may employ fewer workers |
| Better hours and working conditions | Strikes cost workers their pay |
| More job security | Members must pay fees |
| Training raises productivity | Workers may disagree with the union's aims |

This table summarises the November 2023 0455 Paper 21 and June 2026 0455 Paper 23 mark schemes in my own words. The June 2026 scheme capped answers at 3 marks out of 6 if they only listed effects without explaining them.

### Why does union membership rise or fall?

The March 2026 0455 Paper 22 scheme listed reasons both ways. Membership may rise if more people are working, fees fall or working conditions get worse. It may fall if unemployment rises, the law weakens unions or employers give generous pay rises anyway.

The March 2026 report said some candidates defined a trade union correctly but could not build that into a balanced discussion. That is where students get stuck in this whole topic: knowing the facts is not enough, and the marks are in the chain of reasoning.

## Why do some workers earn more than others?

The syllabus gives four reasons for wage differences: demand and supply of labour, bargaining strength, discrimination and government policy. It then asks you to apply them to four comparisons.

| Comparison | Why one group may earn more | Why it may not |
|---|---|---|
| Skilled and unskilled | More training, more productive, harder to replace, so supply is low | Demand for a skill can fall, for example if machines replace it |
| Tertiary and primary sector | Tertiary jobs often need more skill and add more value | Many tertiary jobs, such as cleaning, pay little; dangerous primary jobs such as mining, or high value products such as oil, can pay well |
| Men and women | Discrimination: some employers undervalue women's work | Laws against discrimination can narrow the gap |
| Private and public sector | Private firms may reward productivity with bonuses | Public sector jobs may offer higher pay through set career structures, plus pensions and security |

The rows draw on the June 2022 0455 Paper 21, November 2023 0455 Paper 22 and 23, June 2024 0455 Paper 21 and June 2026 0455 Paper 22 mark schemes.

### A full answer uses demand, supply and bargaining power

When I teach this, I make students explain every wage gap through the labour market. Take a bank's chief executive and a waiter, the comparison in the November 2024 0455 Paper 22 question. That scheme rewarded points such as:

- The chief executive is more qualified, skilled and experienced, so is in short supply.
- Demand for such people is high.
- The chief executive has stronger bargaining power.
- The waiter may belong to a group that faces discrimination, such as migrant workers.

The same scheme gave **no** marks for saying banks are more profitable than restaurants. A cleaner in a bank may still earn less than a waiter, so profit of the employer is not the reason.

<Tip title="Low demand alone is not enough">
The November 2024 0455 Paper 23 mark scheme, on why cleaners earn low wages, did not accept low demand as an answer unless it was compared with high supply. Always explain a low wage as high supply relative to demand: many people can do the job, so firms do not need to pay much to fill it.
</Tip>

### Does inflation explain differences in earnings?

No. In the November 2023 2281 Paper 12 report, a multiple choice question asked why earnings differ between firms in one industry. Only 31% chose the correct answer. Inflation affects every firm in the industry, so it cannot explain a difference between them.

## What is labour mobility?

The November 2024 0455 Paper 21 scheme defined labour mobility as the ability of workers to move to another job. There are two types:

- **Occupational mobility:** moving from one type of job to another. It depends on skills, qualifications and training.
- **Geographical mobility:** moving from one place to another for work. It depends on housing, transport, cost of living and family ties.

The June 2024 0455 Paper 21 scheme also listed age, information about vacancies and discrimination as influences.

How I teach the link to wages: if workers can move easily, a shortage in one job or region draws in workers, so the wage gap narrows. If they cannot move, wage gaps last longer. Higher pay can itself raise mobility: a worker can afford to move house or pay for retraining.

## What is division of labour?

Division of labour means production is split into a series of small jobs, and each worker specialises in one or a few of them. A 2 mark definition needs **both** halves. That is how the 2027 specimen mark scheme splits the marks.

### What are the advantages and disadvantages?

| For | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Firms | Workers get faster and make fewer mistakes, so productivity rises and average cost falls; less training per worker; easier to use machines | Work is disrupted when other staff are absent |
| Workers | Quick to train; skill at one task; productivity may bring higher pay | Repetitive and boring; narrower skills reduce occupational mobility; may be replaced by machines; work disrupted by absent colleagues |

This summarises the June 2025 0455 Paper 22 and November 2025 0455 Paper 23 mark schemes in my words. The November 2025 scheme lists disruption from absent staff as a drawback for workers; I add it for firms too, because lost tasks mean lost output. The June 2025 Paper 22 report praised the best answers on firms, but said few candidates brought in the link to machines. Breaking work into simple, repeated steps makes it easier for a machine to do one of them.

Do not mix it up with specialisation by country. In the June 2022 0455 Paper 23 question on national specialisation, the report said there were candidates who wrote about division of labour instead, such as boredom in factory work, which the question did not ask about.

## How are workers and wages questions marked?

From 2027, Paper 2 is 2 hours and 80 marks, 70% of the qualification. Section A is one compulsory question in six parts. In Section B you answer three of four questions. In the 2027 specimen paper, each Section B question has parts worth 2, 4, 6 and 8 marks.

This topic appears often in Paper 2. Here are some questions from the mark schemes I checked, in my own short wording:

| Paper | Question topic | Marks |
|---|---|---:|
| 0455 March 2022, Paper 22 | Will a national minimum wage reduce poverty? | 8 |
| 0455 June 2022, Paper 21 | Why workers join a trade union | 6 |
| 0455 June 2022, Paper 21 | Are tertiary workers paid more than primary workers? | 8 |
| 0455 November 2022, Paper 22 | Why all teachers' wages may rise | 6 |
| 0455 March 2023, Paper 22 | Why some workers do not join a trade union | 6 |
| 0455 June 2024, Paper 22 | How a minimum wage affects unemployment | 6 |
| 0455 November 2024, Paper 22 | Why a bank chief executive earns more than a waiter | 6 |

| 0455 March 2026, Paper 22 | Will trade union membership rise? | 8 |
| 2027 specimen, Paper 2 | Define division of labour | 2 |

How the parts are marked, from the specimen mark scheme:

- **2 marks (define, identify):** one mark per precise point.
- **4 marks (explain two):** one mark for each reason and one for each explanation.
- **6 marks (analyse):** marks for each logical link in the chain. Where a question says "two reasons", the specimen gave 1 mark for naming each and the rest for analysis.
- **8 marks (discuss):** levels. Level 3 (6 to 8 marks) needs a reasoned argument on both sides, with economic terms used correctly.

Read the exact group the question names. On the teachers question, the November 2022 0455 Paper 22 report said good answers depended on noticing the word **all**: every teacher, not only specialist ones. ## Where do students lose marks on workers and wages?

These come from the examiner reports. In June 2022, the 0455 and 2281 reports carry the same Paper 21 comments.

| Mistake | What to do instead | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Some weak answers wrote as if the trade union were the employer | A union represents workers in bargaining with the employer | June 2022 Paper 21 reports, 0455 and 2281 |
| One sided answers on tertiary and primary pay, or the tertiary reasons simply reversed; a few mixed up primary and secondary | Give separate reasons why primary workers may earn more, such as dangerous work | June 2022 Paper 21 reports, 0455 and 2281 |
| Many could not link higher income to mobility; some thought it meant working faster | More income pays for moving house or for retraining | June 2022 Paper 21 reports, 0455 and 2281 |
| Writing about firms' demand for labour in a mobility question | Mobility is about workers' ability to move: skills, transport, housing | November 2022 0455 Paper 21 report |
| Choosing a geographical reason when asked about occupational immobility | Occupational immobility is about skills; geographical is about place | June 2025 0455 Paper 12 report |
| Taking the biggest dollar gap as the biggest wage discrimination | Compare women's pay as a percentage of men's pay, not the dollar gap | June 2025 0455 Paper 12 report |
| Treating a minimum wage as a minimum price, or giving one side only | It is a wage floor in the labour market; discuss gains and losses | June 2025 0455 Paper 21 report |
| Thinking lower unemployment pushes wages down, or that the number of jobs is fixed | Firms compete for scarce workers, so wages may rise; output can grow | November 2022 0455 Paper 22 report |

On the discrimination question, the June 2025 report gave the figures: 45% chose the correct relative answer and 46% chose the largest absolute gap. On the immobility question, 34% were correct and 58% chose the geographical option.

<Tip title="Name the type of mobility every time">
Write "occupational" or "geographical" in front of the word mobility in every sentence. It stops you mixing the two, and it shows the examiner you know which one the question wants.
</Tip>

## Practice questions with worked answers

Try each one before you read the answer. All three are my own questions, with my own figures.

### Practice question 1

The average weekly wage of food delivery riders in a city rose from $320 to $368.

(a) Calculate the percentage increase in the riders' weekly wage. [2]

(b) Explain one reason why the riders' wage may have risen. [2]

**Answer (a).** Increase = 368 minus 320 = $48. Percentage increase = 48 ÷ 320 × 100 = **15%**. Correct answer: 2 marks. A correct method with a wrong answer would usually earn 1, as in the specimen mark scheme's calculation question.

**Answer (b).** More people order food online (1 mark: reason). The demand for riders is derived from the demand for deliveries, so firms want more riders and compete for them with higher pay (1 mark: explanation).

### Practice question 2

A toy factory splits the making of each toy into ten separate tasks. Explain two disadvantages to the factory of this division of labour. [4]

**Answer.**

- Repeating one task all day can be boring (1). Bored workers lose motivation and make more mistakes, so quality may fall and more toys may be wasted (1).
- Each task depends on the one before it (1). If one worker is absent, the tasks after theirs cannot be done, so output of the whole factory falls (1).

Four marks: one for each disadvantage and one for each explanation.

### Practice question 3

A city starts a large programme to fit solar panels on homes. Analyse how this may affect the wages and the number of electricians employed in the city. [6]

**Answer.**

> Fitting solar panels needs electricians, so demand for electricians rises. This is derived demand, because it comes from demand for the panels (1). The demand for labour curve shifts to the right (1). At the old wage, firms now want more electricians than are available, so there is a shortage (1). Firms compete for the scarce electricians by offering higher pay, so the wage rises (1). The higher wage attracts more people into the work, from other jobs or other areas, so supply extends and more electricians are employed (1). But training takes years, so in the short run supply may rise only a little, and most of the change may be a higher wage rather than many more jobs (1).

Six marks, one for each link in the chain. The last link shows the examiner you know supply cannot always respond quickly.

## FAQ

### Why do some workers earn more than others?

Mainly because of demand and supply of labour. Jobs that need rare skills and long training have low supply, and if demand is high the wage is high. Bargaining power, discrimination and government policy also matter.

### Are trade unions still in the 2027 syllabus?

Yes, inside wage determination. The changes page puts the old separate trade union topic under content removed or significantly reduced, but says trade unions are still referenced in 3.3.2, which lists them and their bargaining power.

### Does a national minimum wage always cause unemployment?

No. If it is set below the equilibrium wage it has no effect. If it is set above, supply of labour can exceed demand, but higher pay may also raise spending and productivity, which can protect jobs.

### What is derived demand for labour?

Firms want workers because consumers want the product those workers make. When demand for the product rises, demand for the workers who make it rises too.

### Is division of labour the same as specialisation?

Division of labour is specialisation by workers within production. The syllabus covers specialisation by country separately, in section 6.1. Keep the two apart in your answers.

### Is this topic the same for IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281?

Yes. Section 3.3 has the same content in both 2027 to 2029 syllabuses, and both have the same Paper 2 structure.
