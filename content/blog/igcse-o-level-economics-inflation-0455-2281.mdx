Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level over time. For IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 you need five things. They are the definition, how the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) measures it, its two causes (demand pull and cost push), its consequences for different groups, and the policies that control it. Each one is below, with model answers.

## What does the syllabus ask you to know about inflation?

Inflation is topic 4.7 in both the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029. The content is the same in both.

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 4.7.1 Definitions of inflation and deflation | Define both terms |
| 4.7.2 Measurement of inflation | Explain how the CPI measures inflation |
| 4.7.3 Causes of inflation | Explain demand pull and cost push inflation |
| 4.7.4 Consequences of inflation | Explain effects on savers, lenders and borrowers, and on consumers, workers, firms and the economy |
| 4.7.5 Policies to control inflation | Explain the policies and judge how well they work |

Deflation now appears only in the 4.7.1 definitions. The syllabus lists the old content about deflation among the content removed or reduced, so learn the definition and do not spend revision time on its causes and consequences. My [0455 syllabus changes article](/blog/igcse-economics-0455-syllabus-2027-changes) lists everything else that moved.

## What is inflation, and how do you define it for 2 marks?

A safe definition has three parts:

1. a **rise**,
2. in the **general price level** (prices across the economy, not one product),
3. **over a period of time** (sustained, not a single jump).

> Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level in an economy over a period of time.

The November 2022 mark scheme for this definition gave marks for the increase, the price level and the time element. The examiner report for that paper said many answers left out time, and many forgot that it is the general level of prices. The weakest answers wrote about the price of one product.

**Deflation** is a sustained fall in the general price level over a period of time. The 2027 syllabus lists only this definition.

### What is the difference between the inflation rate and the price level?

This is a common trap, and examiner reports on Paper 1 multiple choice questions return to it often.

- The **price level** is how expensive things are.
- The **inflation rate** is how fast the price level is rising.

If the inflation rate falls from 6% to 3%, prices are **still rising**, just more slowly. Prices only fall when the inflation rate is negative. The March 2023 examiner report described "a popular misconception that the inflation rate is showing the actual price level".

| Inflation rate this year | What happens to prices |
|---|---|
| Rises from 3% to 6% | Prices rise faster |
| Falls from 6% to 3% | Prices still rise, but more slowly |
| Stays at 4% | Prices keep rising at the same speed |
| Negative, for example minus 2% | Prices fall (deflation) |

A June 2026 question asked students to state what a fall in the inflation rate means. The mark scheme wanted the rate of increase in the price level, and the fact that this rate had fallen.

## How is inflation measured with the Consumer Prices Index?

The **Consumer Prices Index (CPI)** measures changes in the prices of a typical basket of goods and services bought by households. The November 2023 mark scheme gave one mark for saying it measures inflation or the cost of living, and one for mentioning the basket or the weights.

The syllabus names the CPI but does not set out the steps. This is the standard method I teach:

1. **Choose a base year.** Its index number is 100.
2. **Survey households** to find what they buy. This gives the basket.
3. **Give each item a weight.** The weight shows the share of household spending on that item. Food might take 40 out of every 100 spent, so its weight is 40.
4. **Record prices** of the items regularly, in many shops and areas.
5. **Work out a price index for each item**, then multiply each by its weight.
6. **Add the weighted figures and divide by the total weight.** The answer is the CPI.
7. **Compare** the CPI with the year before to get the inflation rate.

The weights matter because a price rise on a big item, such as food, affects households far more than the same rise on a small item.

### Worked example: calculating a weighted price index

Country X uses last year as its base year (index 100). Here are the weights and this year's price index for each item. The figures are my own.

| Item | Weight | Price index this year | Weight × index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 40 | 110 | 4,400 |
| Housing | 30 | 105 | 3,150 |
| Transport | 20 | 120 | 2,400 |
| Other | 10 | 100 | 1,000 |
| **Total** | **100** | | **10,950** |

**Step 1.** Multiply each price index by its weight (the last column).

**Step 2.** Add the weighted figures: 4,400 + 3,150 + 2,400 + 1,000 = 10,950.

**Step 3.** Divide by the total weight: 10,950 ÷ 100 = **109.5**.

**Step 4.** Compare with the base year: 109.5 minus 100 = 9.5, so the inflation rate is **9.5%**.

Notice that transport prices rose the most (20%), yet the CPI rose only 9.5%. Transport carries a weight of only 20, and "Other" did not rise at all.

### What if neither year is the base year?

Use the percentage change formula, not the gap in index points.

Suppose the CPI is 120 in Year 2 and 126 in Year 3.

- Change in index = 126 minus 120 = 6 points.
- Inflation rate = 6 ÷ 120 × 100 = **5%**.

Writing "6%" here loses the mark. The gap in points only equals the percentage when the earlier year is the base year at 100.

### What are the limits of the CPI?

The syllabus does not list these. They are standard points I teach, because they help you judge inflation figures:

- The basket is an average. A household that spends more on food than the weights suggest faces a different inflation rate.
- Buying habits change, and the basket and weights are updated only from time to time.
- Better quality products may cost more, and the CPI may count that as inflation.

## What causes inflation?

The syllabus names two causes. Learn both chains of reasoning, because mark schemes credit the chain, not the label alone.

| Feature | Demand pull inflation | Cost push inflation |
|---|---|---|
| Starts with | Total demand rising faster than total supply | Costs of production rising |
| Firms respond by | Raising prices because buyers want more than can be produced | Raising prices to protect their profit margins |
| Typical triggers | Lower income tax, higher government spending, lower interest rates, more bank lending, a rise in exports, higher confidence | Higher wages not matched by higher productivity, dearer raw materials or energy, a fall in the exchange rate making imports dearer, higher indirect taxes, cuts to subsidies |
| Most likely when | The economy is close to full employment | Any time costs rise, even with weak demand |

### Demand pull inflation in detail

Total demand is all spending in the economy: households, firms, the government and foreign buyers of exports. When it rises, firms try to produce more. If they have spare workers and machines, output rises and prices may barely move.

Near full employment, firms cannot raise output quickly. Buyers compete for the same goods, so prices are pulled up. That is why exam questions link a fall in unemployment to demand pull inflation.

A larger money supply can also feed demand pull inflation. More money and easier borrowing mean more spending, and if output cannot keep up, prices rise.

### Cost push inflation in detail

Firms pass higher costs on to buyers as higher prices. The main sources are wages, raw materials, energy and imported inputs.

Wages are the classic case. If workers win a pay rise but produce no more per hour, the cost of each unit rises and firms raise prices. Workers then see their pay buys less and ask for another rise. This is a **wage price spiral**: higher prices lead to higher wage claims, which lead to higher prices again.

The exchange rate also matters. A fall in the value of a currency makes imported raw materials and machinery more expensive, which pushes up firms' costs.

<Tip title="Always name the type of inflation">
When a question asks how something causes inflation, write "demand pull inflation" or "cost push inflation", never just "inflation". The June 2026 mark scheme for a question on the exchange rate and inflation told examiners not to credit the bare word "inflation".
</Tip>

### Why do countries have different inflation rates?

The June 2023 reports for both 0455 and 2281 noted that many candidates could not explain this. They described what high or low inflation looks like instead of what causes it. Answer with causes. One country may have faster growth in total demand, stronger wage pressure, or a government that has cut taxes or raised spending.

## What are the consequences of inflation?

The syllabus splits this into two lists. Questions often name one group, so always write about the group in the question.

### Savers, lenders and borrowers

Inflation reduces the **real value of money**: each dollar buys less.

| Group | Effect of inflation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Savers | Usually lose | If the interest rate is below the inflation rate, savings buy less when withdrawn |
| Lenders | Usually lose | The money repaid buys less than the money lent |
| Borrowers | Usually gain | The real value of what they owe falls |

A quick example with my own figures. Amina saves $1,000 at an interest rate of 4%. After a year she has $1,040. Over the same year inflation was 6%, so a basket that cost $1,000 now costs $1,060. Her savings grew, but they buy less than before. Borrowers are the mirror image: they repay money that is worth less.

The November 2023 examiner report for 2281 explained a multiple choice question the same way: past borrowers gain because their repayments are now worth less.

### Consumers, workers, firms and the economy

| Group | Possible harm | Possible gain or limit to the harm |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Real incomes fall if prices rise faster than incomes; the poor are hit hardest when the prices of essentials rise | Little harm if incomes rise as fast as prices |
| Workers | Real wages fall; workers on fixed pay lose most | Strong trade unions may win pay rises that keep pace |
| Firms | Higher costs of raw materials, wages and capital goods; **menu costs** (the cost of changing prices); harder to plan; may lose sales abroad | Demand pull inflation can raise sales and revenue; the real value of firms' debts falls |
| The economy | Exports become less price competitive if inflation is higher than in rival countries; uncertainty can reduce investment; possible current account deficit | Low and stable inflation is rarely a problem and may come with growth |

Two more terms mark schemes credit:

- **Shoe leather costs**: the time and effort people spend shopping around for lower prices.
- **Fiscal drag**: as money incomes rise with inflation, people are pulled into higher income tax bands, so they pay more tax even though their real income has not risen.

The size of inflation matters. Low, steady inflation causes few problems. Very high inflation, or inflation that is hard to predict, causes the most damage because nobody can plan. The June 2022 examiner report noted that strong answers told the difference between creeping inflation (slow and steady) and hyperinflation (extremely fast).

## What policies can control inflation?

The policies come from topics 4.2, 4.3 and 4.4 of the syllabus. Most reduce inflation by reducing total demand. Supply side policies work the other way, by raising total supply.

| Policy | Measures to reduce inflation | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Contractionary fiscal policy | Raise taxes, cut government spending | Less disposable income and less public spending reduce total demand |
| Contractionary monetary policy | Raise the interest rate, reduce the money supply, allow a higher exchange rate | Borrowing costs more and saving pays more, so spending falls; a higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper and reduces demand for exports |
| Supply side policy | Education and training, infrastructure spending, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, better incentives to work and invest, privatisation | Raises productivity and productive capacity, so costs fall and output can grow without prices rising |

Keep the policies with the right body. Fiscal policy is set by the government. Monetary policy is usually run by the central bank. The March 2023 examiner report noted candidates who wrote about the central bank using fiscal policy.

### How effective are these policies?

This is what the 8 mark "discuss" questions test. Use these points:

1. **Match the policy to the cause.** Contractionary fiscal and monetary policy suit demand pull inflation. If inflation is cost push, cutting demand mostly reduces output and jobs while costs stay high.
2. **Conflicts with other aims.** The syllabus lists full employment and stable prices as a possible conflict. Lower demand can mean higher unemployment and slower economic growth.
3. **Time.** Supply side policies such as education take years to raise productivity. In the short run, the extra spending on them can even add to total demand.
4. **Side effects.** A higher interest rate discourages investment, which can harm future growth. Higher taxes reduce incentives to work.
5. **Size and confidence.** A small rise in the interest rate may not change spending if households and firms are confident.

### Can a policy make inflation worse?

Yes. A June 2023 multiple choice question turned on this, and many candidates chose the wrong option. A rise in indirect taxes raises prices directly, so it does not reduce the inflation rate even though it is a tax rise. Cuts to subsidies raise firms' costs and can cause cost push inflation. A cut in the interest rate encourages borrowing and spending, so it is more likely to raise inflation than reduce it.

## How are inflation questions marked in Paper 2?

From 2027, Paper 2 is 2 hours and 80 marks. Section A is one compulsory 20 mark question on unseen data. In Section B you answer three questions out of four, each worth 20 marks in parts (a) to (d). In the 2027 specimen paper, those parts are worth 2, 4, 6 and 8 marks. The table below follows the specimen mark scheme.

| Part | Command word | How marks are given |
|---|---|---|
| (a) 2 marks | Define, identify, state | Precise points |
| (b) 4 marks | Explain, often two reasons | 1 for each reason, 1 for each explanation |
| (c) 6 marks | Analyse | Marks for each logical step in the chain |
| (d) 8 marks | Discuss | Levels: Level 1 (1 to 2) undeveloped points, Level 2 (3 to 5) some analysis but may be one sided, Level 3 (6 to 8) both sides developed |

### Model answer: an "explain two" question

**Question (mine):** Explain two causes of cost push inflation. [4]

> **Higher wages.** If workers receive pay rises that are larger than any rise in their productivity, the cost of producing each unit rises, so firms raise prices to protect their profits.
>
> **Higher import prices.** If the country's currency falls in value, imported raw materials become more expensive, which raises firms' costs of production and so the prices they charge.

Each paragraph names the cause (1 mark) and explains how it raises prices (1 mark).

### Model answer: an "analyse" question

**Question (mine):** Analyse how a cut in income tax may cause inflation. [6]

> A cut in income tax increases households' disposable income. With more income to spend, consumer spending rises, so total demand in the economy increases. If the economy is close to full employment, firms cannot increase output quickly enough to meet this extra demand. Buyers compete for the limited goods, so firms raise prices, causing demand pull inflation.
>
> Higher demand for products also raises firms' demand for workers. If workers are scarce, firms must offer higher wages to attract them. If wages rise faster than productivity, costs of production rise, and firms pass these on as higher prices, causing cost push inflation.

The answer never jumps a stage. The March 2025 examiner report gave this exact advice: do not jump from wage rises straight to lower profits; explain that higher wages raise costs unless productivity rises too.

### Model answer: a "discuss" question

**Question (mine):** Discuss whether or not raising the interest rate is an effective way to control inflation. [8]

> **Why it might.** A higher interest rate makes borrowing more expensive, so households take out fewer loans to buy cars and houses, and firms borrow less to invest. Saving also earns more, so people spend less of their income. Consumer spending and investment fall, so total demand falls. With less demand pressing on the same output, firms have less reason to raise prices, so demand pull inflation falls. A higher interest rate may also raise the exchange rate. Imported raw materials then become cheaper, which reduces firms' costs and so cost push inflation.
>
> **Why it might not.** If inflation is caused by rising costs, such as higher energy prices, a higher interest rate does little to reduce those costs. Firms that borrow face higher interest payments, which adds to their costs. Households and firms that are confident about the future may keep spending anyway. The policy also takes time to work, and the fall in demand may raise unemployment, which conflicts with the aim of full employment.
>
> **Judgement.** A higher interest rate is most likely to reduce inflation when inflation is demand pull and the rise is large enough to change spending. If inflation is cost push, it may cut output and jobs while prices keep rising, so supply side measures would be needed alongside it.

This is written to meet the Level 3 description: both sides are developed with chains of reasoning, it uses the right terms, and the judgement says what the answer depends on.

## Where do students lose marks on inflation?

These come from the 0455 and 2281 examiner reports from 2022 to 2025, plus one June 2026 mark scheme.

| Mistake | What to do instead | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Definition misses "general" price level or the time element | Write all three parts: rise, general price level, over time | 0455 November 2022 report |
| Treating a fall in the inflation rate as a fall in prices | A lower positive rate means prices still rise, just more slowly | 0455 March 2022 and March 2023 reports; 2281 November 2023 report |
| Writing "inflation" without the type | Name demand pull or cost push inflation and explain the chain | 0455 June 2026 mark scheme (not a report) |
| Jumping stages in a chain of reasoning | Write each link: wage rise, higher costs, unless productivity rises, narrower profit gap | 0455 March 2025 report |
| Writing about the whole economy when the question names firms | Keep every point on the group in the question | 0455 March 2025 report |
| Describing high or low inflation instead of explaining its causes | Give causes: demand growth, wage pressure, import prices, policy | 0455 and 2281 June 2023 reports |
| Saying the central bank uses fiscal policy | Central bank: interest rates and money supply; government: taxes and spending | 0455 March 2023 report |
| Confusing income tax with taxes on goods | Higher income tax reduces demand and inflation; higher indirect tax raises prices | 0455 and 2281 June 2022 reports |

<Tip title="Read the group in the question twice">
"Effects of inflation on firms", "on workers" and "on the economy" are three different questions. Underline the group before you write, and start every paragraph with it.
</Tip>

## Can you answer these practice questions?

Try each one before you read the answer. All three are my own questions.

**1.** The CPI was 140 last year and 147 this year. Calculate the inflation rate. [2]

**Answer.** Change = 147 minus 140 = 7 points. Inflation rate = 7 ÷ 140 × 100 = **5%**. Writing 7% would lose the mark, because last year was not the base year.

**2.** Explain two ways in which firms may benefit when inflation is low and stable. [4]

**Answer.** First, the country's products stay price competitive abroad (1), so firms can sell more exports and lose fewer sales to imports (1). Second, stable prices make the future more certain (1), so firms find it easier to plan and are more willing to invest (1).

**3.** Analyse how a rise in the world price of oil may cause inflation in a country that imports all its oil. [6]

**Answer.** Oil is used to make fuel and electricity and to transport goods, so almost every firm depends on it. When the world price rises, the country pays more for the same amount of imported oil. Firms' energy and transport costs rise, so the cost of producing each unit increases. To protect their profit margins, firms raise the prices of their products. The higher costs reach firms across the whole economy, so the general price level rises. This is cost push inflation. Households also pay more for fuel, so workers may ask for higher wages to keep up, which raises firms' costs again.

## FAQ

### Is inflation always bad?

No. Low and stable inflation causes few problems and often comes with a growing economy. Inflation does most harm when it is high or hard to predict, because people and firms cannot plan.

### What is the difference between demand pull and cost push inflation?

Demand pull inflation starts with total demand rising faster than total supply, so prices are pulled up. Cost push inflation starts with rising costs of production, which firms pass on as higher prices.

### Who gains from inflation?

Borrowers usually gain, because the real value of what they owe falls. Firms may gain if demand pull inflation raises their sales faster than their costs.

### Do I still need to learn deflation for 2027?

Only the definition. The 2027 to 2029 syllabus lists the definitions of inflation and deflation in 4.7.1, and it lists the old content about deflation among the content removed or reduced.

### Is inflation the same in 0455 and 2281?

Yes. Topic 4.7 has the same content in the IGCSE 0455 and O Level 2281 syllabuses for 2027 to 2029, and both are tested in Paper 1 and Paper 2.

### Which policy is best for controlling inflation?

It depends on the cause. Contractionary fiscal and monetary policy suit demand pull inflation. Supply side policy suits cost push inflation in the long run, but it works slowly.
